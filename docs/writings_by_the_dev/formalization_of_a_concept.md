Formalization of a Concept
As we delve into understanding the fundamental threads of knowledge, a pursuit that has intrigued humans since ancient times, it appears that concepts are resolving to be the quintessential units of comprehension, forming the cornerstone of thought and knowledge. They serve as the elemental particles from which the complex molecules of thought are constructed. Within the realm of understanding, we see that concepts manifest in distinct frameworks, each with the potential to be formalized, revealing patterns and rules in how they link to one another.
To develop a more intuitive grasp of the nature of understanding, let's consider how we obtain this state in common life endeavors. For instance, learning to ride a bicycle involves grasping the coordination of motions and balance necessary to control the bicycle's movement through space. If you aim to learn some skill like knitting, you are achieving an understanding how a dance of motions between the yarn and two needles can achieve a repetitive knotting that stacks on itself to create a growing plane of fuzzy material which can be conformed to various designs for various purposes. Each design pattern is an artifact of knowledge that must be learned meaning an understanding is reached as to what series of steps achieves the design. It is a framework in which given a particular state of progression through the creation of the knitting the knowledge of the scope of meaning that is the design can be recalled in order to inform as to what the next action needs to be.
However, it's important to note that not all aspects of a process are essential to understand for achieving the end result. In knitting, for instance, one might not fully grasp the physics behind why certain patterns cause the material to curl or fold. While these nuances contribute to a richer understanding, they are not always essential for achieving the practical outcome of recreating a specific design. The particulars of why a certain knotting pattern becomes the design you desire is a level of understanding that is not required to just merely recreate the steps that result in it. This selective depth in learning demonstrates that understanding can occur at various levels, from surface-level replication of actions to deeper comprehension of underlying principles. From this example we can see that learning has many resolutions and scopes in which something can be learned. Understanding a sequence of steps that lead to an outcome is in itself a concept where the deeper fundamental mechanics that cause the design or fabric curling are separate concepts. Learning is merely the act of increasing an understanding of some scope of patternization that exists.

If the expanse of your understanding halts at the boundary of which steps achieve a particular design. You will be able to utilize you understanding in order to replicate the specific knitting piece. But that knowledge doesn’t inform us as to what other forms can be made or if there is something incorrect about the design and how to reverse action and resolve the error. For this a richer comprehension for more fundamental concepts as to the knit design are required. Where if you do achieve an understanding as to what the mechanics are that cause the fabric to curl it can be utilized to other forms beyond this one specific design. Or knowing the fundamentals as to why a specific combination of knots in a specific order results in the design gives agency as to to reverse and correct the errors as you will be able to dissect why and where the required knitting states took the wrong course. But yet knowing all of physics and secrets of the universe and only knowing the knowledge of the steps that yield the design are both equally capable knowledge bases at the specific task of creating a scarf of a particular aesthetic. For a specific outcome there is a minimal base of understanding that is needed to where any more robust knowledge base will not be able to outperform.

So the space of concepts is a hierarchical nature where a concept exists in a tree of endless others. Using our knitting example, achieving the desired knit pattern was a result of reaching an understanding of many concepts, a conceptual understanding of the needles, the yarn, a pattern of wrist and finger motion, even very primitively a concept of space, and time itself is at the foundation of the end concept of the scarf that we create. The concepts seem to live at these points of contact of relationships between the fragments of all possible states and dynamics.

A concept chain symbolizes a linear progression of understanding; it is inherently sequential, where comprehension of one idea is a prerequisite for the next. Envision a path marked from 'A' to 'D' (A->B->C->D), where each step is a necessary precursor to the next; you cannot grasp 'B' without first internalizing 'A', nor 'C' without 'B', culminating in the eventual understanding of 'D'. The journey from 'A' to 'D' is methodical: one cannot leap to 'D' without trekking through the preceding concepts. This methodical progression is akin to unlocking the doors of knowledge one after the other, where each step thoughtfully unveils a piece of the broader picture. It is this growing comprehension of how units of meaning interrelate that determines the level of abstraction at which we can contemplate.
The more effectively we can distill a concept to its fundamental essence, the smoother our journey across the conceptual landscape becomes.This ability to deconstruct complex ideas into their fundamental components is key to constructing clear pathways through the terrain of understanding. The learning process often mirrors this very exploration: it is a quest in search of the next concept that fits perfectly into our existing mental framework, thus propelling us forward to the subsequent phase of comprehension. This conceptual expedition embodies the core of a systematic learning trajectory, where the speed and depth of acquiring knowledge are as much influenced by the logical sequence of concepts presented as by the content itself.
Consider the game of chess as an illustrative example of a concept chain. Understanding the game requires navigating along a chain of concepts where each one acts as a necessary checkpoint in which to access the next. This journey of understanding begins with the first concept (A), the chessboard – an 8x8 grid where the game takes place. Understanding the board's layout is crucial for subsequent concepts. Next (B) comes a general understanding of piece movement rules. Here, it’s essential to recognize that each chess piece has specific movement rules and occupies one of the 64 spaces on the board, though the precise details of each piece’s movements aren’t required initially.
The third concept (C) in this chain is 'check,' occurring when a king is under threat of capture based on an opposing piece’s movement capabilities. This concept hinges on the understanding that the opposing piece's position allows its movement rules to influence the king's location directly. Finally, the culmination of this chain is the concept of 'checkmate' (D). 'Checkmate' arises when the king is in check and no legal moves remain to alleviate this state. The concept of checkmate lives at a higher abstraction than check in which is a higher abstraction from piece movement and so on. The abstraction of a concept is the degree in which it relies on other concepts for its existence and a concept chain is the skeletal path of incremental progression into abstraction.
This progression from understanding the chessboard and piece movements to grasping strategic concepts like check and checkmate demonstrates the sequential nature of conceptual comprehension. Between these key concepts lie potentially endless intermediate ideas, such as the distinct role of the king and the mechanics of capturing pieces. The degree a concept chain is a continuum or has distinct increments is unknown but between any two concepts along a chain we distinguish there exists many other potential conceptual points to highlight. For instance, A, B and C are not intended to be an exhaustive framing of concept D’s foundation but rather unavoidable choke points that must be traversed to the eventual end of reaching D. Additionally, concept D becomes a checkpoint in which to access even further abstract ideas. Understanding 'checkmate' unlocks concepts such as "Back Rank Mate" or "Smothered Mate." For each of these advanced concepts, a thorough understanding of 'checkmate' was a prerequisite. Each branches from this common node in the concept chain, diverging only after reaching the foundational concept of 'checkmate.'
Furthermore, this branching of concepts creates intricate trees of relationships at every critical juncture along the chain. For instance, the concept of 'check' serves as a parent node for understanding a 'hard pin,' a situation where a piece cannot move without placing its king in check. Where a ‘soft pin’ is a derivative of the concept ‘capture’ in which ‘check’ roots. Placing its existence somewhere between ‘B’ and ‘C’. A concept chain has meaning in relation to a specified end concept. Where different concepts may have overlapping chains yet each concept ultimately has its own unique lineage of prerequisite parent concepts necessary for its full understanding.
The universe of concepts, as seen in chess, is highly branching. Yet, for any given concept, there exists a chain of essential preceding concepts that must be internalized in a specific order. Much like the essential economy required for manufacturing a product, where a series of tasks must be sequentially completed, every concept demands an understanding of its foundational concepts. It's important to recognize that while this example does not provide an exhaustive list of concepts leading to the understanding of 'checkmate,' it illustrates a critical pathway: the concept of 'checkmate' is predicated on understanding 'check,' which in turn depends on grasping piece movement, itself reliant on comprehending the chessboard's spatial layout. There may be a continuous amount of concepts in between A and B as described in this example. Regardless how many incremental concepts may be possibly feasible for distillation these are essential checkpoints that must be reached before the highlighted next abstract concept is possibly obtainable.
This chain implies that for any complex concept to be fully understood, certain essential checkpoints must be reached, regardless of the path taken. To draw a parallel, consider the journey to an airport: no matter the route, passing through security is an unavoidable checkpoint before boarding a plane. The concepts chosen are necessary concept checkpoints that must be reached regardless of the route to reaching them. The concept chain is highlighting that a degree of abstraction is unattainable without an essential parent concept, in which itself has an essential parent concept.
Concept Towers
Concept towers present a distinct framework in the realm of concepts, where the comprehension of a higher-level concept hinges on a foundation built from multiple underlying concepts. In this model, each individual concept—let's call them A, B, C, and D—can be understood in its own right, each residing within its unique pathway or chain in the conceptual landscape. However, the understanding of a more complex concept, say E, necessitates a foundational knowledge of all these elements—A, B, C, and D. Collectively, they form the base upon which concept E is erected.
This structure differs fundamentally from a concept chain. While a concept chain signifies a linear progression of understanding, leading step by step to a desired conceptual endpoint, a concept tower emphasizes the necessity of simultaneous foundational concepts. Unlike a concept chain, where each concept is linked to a single preceding node, a concept tower's apex concept (E) is anchored in multiple foundational concepts. This means several parallel concept chains must be navigated to reach a point where a new, more complex concept, which relies on the convergence of these different paths, becomes comprehensible. Essentially, concept E materializes only when there's a simultaneous grasp of multiple diverse concepts, each contributing to its formation.
An example of a concept tower can be seen in understanding the concept of a bicycle. To fully grasp what a bicycle is, one must simultaneously understand several independent sub-concepts and how they interrelate into the scope of concept we are considering. For instance, the wheel, motion, and the aspects of human anatomy, such as how our legs move the pedals or hands steer the handlebars. Each of these concepts exists in their own independent branches in concept space, yet they must be understood collectively to fully comprehend what a bicycle is. A bicycle only resolves a meaning when a relationship of its subconcepts coalesce a relationship into the same point of meaning. Stated more precisely, the nodes of each sub concept each have an edge that connects to the same coordinates in concept space.
In this conceptual structure, each sub-concept acts as a foundational pillar, supporting the overarching concept of the bicycle. The complete idea of a bicycle materializes only when a foundational base comprising these sub-concepts are combined. For instance, while the addition of a basket on the handlebars contributes to the concept, it's not an essential component for understanding what a bicycle is. The absence of this element doesn't hinder the fundamental comprehension of a bicycle.
This illustrates how a broader base of foundational concepts offers more combinatorial possibilities to form more abstract concepts. The more breadth of a concept graph we form we  increase the likelihood of accumulating  the minimal essential base required for understanding a more complex idea. As we accumulate more foundational concepts, we enhance our capacity to reach new levels of abstraction. Subsequently, these newly formed concepts can themselves become the base for even more advanced concepts, contributing to an ever-growing and evolving tower of conceptual understanding. This ongoing construction of concept towers leads to an expanding comprehension of increasingly abstract ideas.
In the context of a concept tower, if we can identify the necessary base concepts for a higher-level idea, we can develop a more systematic approach to teaching and learning. This approach would resemble less of an abstract art form and more of a structured methodology transposing into Neo learning Kung Fu in 'The Matrix'. By understanding the foundational concepts required for a complex idea, we can create a roadmap or checklist that guides us through the necessary stages of comprehension, paving the way for a more effective and efficient acquisition of knowledge.

Concepts and Learning
The objective of learning is to gain an understanding of relationships within a specific domain of meaning. For example, learning to ride a bicycle involves comprehending the combination of motions and balance necessary to control the bicycle's movement through space. Similarly, in learning a skill like knitting, you learn how a dance of motions between yarn and two needles can achieve a repetitive knotting in the yarn that stacks on itself to create a growing plane of fuzzy material which can be conformed to various designs for various purposes. The sequence of steps that lead to the manifestation of a particular pattern is a piece of knowledge, a concept in its own right that results in a desired outcome. This approach represents a direct, straightforward understanding that compresses the complexities of knitting into a simpler form.
In contrast, a more profound level of understanding delves into the mechanics of knitting: the properties of the yarn, the physics behind each stitch, and the reasons certain patterns emerge from specific movements. This deeper comprehension resides at a different conceptual altitude, equipping the learner not just to follow a pattern but to understand, adapt, and even create it. It's an expansive form of learning that transcends mere memorization, encompassing a broader grasp of the principles that govern the skill. These details are standalone pieces of knowledge that can be learned independently. Yet, for the purpose of recreating a specific design, a basic understanding of the steps leading to the right knotting is sufficient. The finer nuances of why those patterns result in the desired design might not be necessary for replication.
This distinction illustrates that learning can occur at multiple levels of resolution. One might learn just enough to replicate a design, or delve deeper into the underlying principles to gain a richer understanding. Learning is merely the act of increasing an understanding of some scale of patternization that exists.
This contrast highlights the nature of learning as being in reference to a scope of detail, where understanding can range from surface-level replication to in-depth comprehension. The chosen depth of learning is influenced by the learner’s needs and goals, demonstrating that learning is in reference to what agency is required for a specific objective. The more rich a conceptual architecture you come to understand, the cone of problem solving you have agency to address grows.
