Language
Formalizing language
Numerous methodologies and theories have been proposed to decipher the mechanics of language, many seeking to identify the significant units of meaning language assembles to communicate information. However, for every plausible structure suggested, exceptions seem to emerge, challenging these proposed frameworks. For instance, the concept of morphemes and phonemes as the written and spoken fundamental units of meaning is contradicted by the observation that identical meanings can be conveyed despite variations in accents. The pronunciation of 'car' in a Boston accent as “C-ah” and in a more common accent illustrates this: fundamentally different phonetic elements, yet conveying the same meaning.  Furthermore, colloquial contractions like transforming “want to?” into “wanna?” and countless other variations in expressions demonstrate that meaning in language is not rigidly tied to specific phonetic or morphemic structures. This suggests that language is more dynamic than a mere assemblage of symbolic pieces crafted to convey precise meanings.
Additionally, these theories often explore whether the mind adheres to specific rules for inflecting words into different morphologies, such as transforming verbs into past tense by adding “ed,” or pluralizing nouns with “s.” This pattern repetition in language suggests a rule-based system underpinning its structure. Yet, this theoretical framework is challenged by the presence of exceptions. Irregular word forms, such as 'ran' as the past tense of 'run' or 'mice' as the plural of 'mouse', defy standard morphological patterns. Following conventional rules would incorrectly produce forms like 'runned' and 'mouses'. These exceptions challenge our understanding of language structure. The conundrum intensifies as we consider the fluidity and contextual usage of these irregular forms. Raising questions about the mechanisms that allow for their seamless integration into effective communication without additional cognitive overhead. How does our cognition effortlessly integrate and recognize these exceptions, forming meaningful language constructs without explicit clarification?
This dilemma underlines the elusive nature of language mechanics. Our understanding of language is not only clouded by its intrinsic complexity but also by the methodologies we adopt to decode it. Language, in its essence, may be more dynamic and less bound to rigid structures than we previously assumed. The challenge lies in recognizing the subtleties and nuances that escape the confines of established linguistic theories, suggesting a more dynamic and situational system is at play in language formation and comprehension.
When exploring the mechanics of a tangible, mechanical system we can often reverse-engineer the process. We can trace the movement of gears back to their original energy source, understand the series of kinetic inputs, and ultimately reveal how the hands of a watch rotate. However, when it comes to language, the mechanics are far more complex and less observable. The neural network of the human brain, with its intricate web of wiring, and layers of communication through chemical means, still lacks clear understanding in terms of minute cause and effect. We have yet to grasp fully the intricacies behind the firing of specific neuron patterns and how these relate to our actions and behavior – what memories are retained or discarded, why one word is chosen over another, or how momentary emotions influence our responses. Despite significant advancements in neuroscience, revealing remarkable insights about the brain and mind, the underlying mechanics of how we use and process language remain shrouded in complexity.
The entanglement of this story of complexity and the mechanics of language suggest that our current approaches to understanding language might be limited. Traditional linguistic theories tend to dissect the mechanics of language, focusing on the 'how' its functioning. However, this approach might be akin to examining the gears of a watch without acknowledging its purpose – to tell time. Similarly, in studying language, we might be scrutinizing its structural elements without fully appreciating the 'what' language is doing. A reorientation in methodology may be needed, one that identifies the broader functions and purposes of language before attempting to unravel its mechanics. The primary question, 'What is language accomplishing?' should precede detangling its execution. Such a shift in perspective could provide essential context, to then give a clear objective for the mechanical intricacies to language to have reference to. Progress and insight may be more easily accessible by merely constructing the right framing in which to consider the complexities of language.

However, as society evolved and these roles became obsolete, the language that once vibrated with life and utility began to atrophy. The words and phrases that were once pivotal in everyday communication became relics, their use dwindling as the generations that needed them passed on. Like limbs succumbing to frostbite, these segments of our linguistic heritage darkened and fell silent, their expressions and the concepts they represented becoming dormant remnants of a past era. The changing dynamics of society and culture is a constant stream of leaving terms as stranded withering dendritic vestiges of the past and new ones spouting as there are new dynamics of reality in which to communicate.  Almost akin to a type of linguistic temperature or velocity of communication. The frequency at which a term is used may be a metric as to the significance a corresponding concept plays in the collective mind of a population. Terms that outlast eras of societal progression might imply a degree of foundationness to their conceptual counterpart as pillars of societal cohesion.

These nuanced distinctions lack direct equivalents in Western cultures, highlighting the specificity of language to environmental and cultural contexts. Vocabulary dynamically emerges from the details of our lives and environments that have practical use in being communicated.

It demonstrates how language adapts, creating and discarding terms based on the shifting needs and priorities of its speakers. Utility of communication is the force of natural selection acting on a body of vocabulary. Where different ways of life necessitate different details of reality to convey to one another and a different overlay of terminology results.
This demonstrates that the relationship between our language and the full spectrum of known concepts isn't a perfect one-to-one match but rather a selective overlap, dictated by the segments of conceptual space deemed most relevant for communication over time. The evolution of our lexicon is a sparse projection of the changes of societal priorities and environmental interactions. Despite this partial coverage, the connection between the substrate of concepts and the domain of language is unmistakably interlinked.
Framing Language
This framing aims to highlight the entangled interplay between our vocabulary and the territory of concepts we find use in communicating with one another. By delving into the nuanced utility of our language's lexicon and its interaction with the conceptual foundation, we revisit the core inquiry of this discourse: What is language and what is it doing? To address this, I propose a framework to infer the practical application of our lexical resources. In essence, language functions as a mechanism for the conveyance of concepts. At its core it is an information system, which is a vague statement but nonetheless critical to acknowledge.
Information by definition implies the reduction of uncertainty, and in essence results from an acknowledgement of what isn’t rather than what is. Emerging more from a recognition of absence rather than presence. It clarifies areas of ambiguity, continually unveiling closer approximations to the inherent characteristics of a given space. In instances of pure deduction identifying what is not is directly equivalent to identifying what is. In most cases however, this is not the outcome and we instead shape closer approximations rather than absolute delineations. Acknowledging language as a mechanism for the purpose of information exchange prompts the question: What specific uncertainties does it seek to resolve? I contend that language operates fundamentally as a navigational tool. Providing direction through a labyrinth of relational pathways towards a specific conceptual goal. It systematically diminishes ambiguity, transmitting bits of information in which aim to reduce the uncertainty as to which relational edge navigates closer to the destination concept that is intended to be shared.
When we communicate, there is an ultimate idea that you wish a listener or reader to understand and arrive at. Where the verbiage of “arrive” may be more literal a description than analogous, suggesting a navigation through a conceptual landscape. I describe the territory of concepts as being arranged in a network substrate. Imagine this landscape as a vast network of interconnected concepts, similar to a map of roads and destinations. Each concept is a node, linked by edges representing their relationships. For instance, the concept of "Pizza" isn't standalone but connects with "tomato sauce," "dough," and "cheese," each an independent node within this network. These connections are pathways through which language directs us, aiming to reduce uncertainty as to what path to take next. View each edge as a literal road or path that can be traversed and language as a mechanism that conveys information as to which junctions to take.
Consider standing at a crossroads in a dense forest, faced with multiple paths: left, right, or straight ahead. Language acts as our guide, signaling the direction to take to avoid getting lost. The information embedded in our language I propose aims to dispel uncertainty about which direction to take at each junction along our pathing through the conceptual graph. From pizza, we might explore various toppings, or branch into related territories like savory pies, Italian cuisine, or cooking methods. Each new node we encounter is connected to others, forming a web of knowledge that, when navigated effectively, brings us closer to our intended destination.
Thus, mastering language is akin to mastering navigation through this conceptual space. Learning the mechanics and fundamentals of language might then blend and converge with the solution space of path finding. Just as a map and series of directions helps us find our way through a city's streets, language helps us chart a course through complex networks of thought, from one concept to another. By using the edges as our pathways between locations, we can communicate the navigation through concept space visiting intermittent concepts between our starting point and an eventual goal concept. This I propose is the high level idea as to what language is and is doing.
Navigation
To investigate the premise that language serves as a navigation system, it's imperative to first establish the criteria that define such systems. Asserting that language embodies navigational functions requires a precise examination of the essential characteristics that constitute this category of system. Once these criteria are articulated, we have a benchmark template to reference in order to accurately evaluate whether language exhibits the requisite traits for this classification. The foundational elements are defined as follows:
Establishing both an origin and target: The joint identification of both an origin and target position or goal must be established. An origin offers a reference for the onset of movement while a target defines the criteria for successful arrival. These two elements jointly anchor the holistic navigation process with direction and purpose.
Directional Guidance Mechanism: This component facilitates the decision-making process for selecting a movement direction from available options, coupled with the capability to actualize decisions into movement. It employs a heuristic methodology of making choices that over a sequence of steps collectively bridge the origin and target.
Recognition of Arrival: Lastly, a method of discerning the fulfillment of the target's criteria is needed, signaling a point where further decisions on direction or movement end, thereby indicating the navigation's conclusion.
These foundational elements ensure that a navigation system is equipped to fulfill its primary function of guiding from a point of origin to a desired goal. While additional features may enhance the system's efficiency and adaptability, the essence of navigation lies within these core components.
Now that we have established a template for navigation’s essential attributes we can define a navigation system as one that embodies these key elements. Thereby stating that language is a system of navigation we are stating that it is a system capable of establishing origin and target, choose among potential paths, and signal the achievement of the target.  If language can meet the criteria set forth by each essential element, it supports the assertion that it functions as a navigation system. Therefore, understanding its mechanics are to relate how each mechanical detail facilitates the function of navigation.

History of graph navigation
To investigate a method discoverable by evolution or natural selection to reliably find paths through networks and communicate information about this pathing, it is worthwhile to examine the history and evolution of graph theory and the methods developed to infer insights about various graphs. This examination aims to demonstrate that graphs are an inherent framework for navigation. We can potentially utilize the progress made in algorithmic methods for this purpose and apply it to the utility of language, if not precisely, then at least comparatively.
The story begins with a renowned mathematician you are likely familiar with, either by name or through contributions such as the numeric value of e and various other mathematical constructs: Leonhard Euler. Euler is credited with being the first to conceive and utilize a graph to model relationships in the world. He addressed a recreational puzzle in the German city of Königsberg, which involved determining whether it was possible to traverse all the city's seven bridges exactly once and return to the starting point.
Königsberg had seven bridges connecting various parts of the city, including two islands. Euler represented the landmasses as nodes and the bridges as edges connecting these nodes. By reducing the problem to this graphical abstraction, he systematically analyzed potential paths and outcomes. He discovered that it was impossible to traverse all the bridges exactly once and return to the starting point because the graph was such that not all nodes had an even degree, which is a requirement for an Eulerian circuit to exist. Furthermore, an Eulerian path which travels each edge exactly once but isn’t required to begin and end at the same node is also not possible because there are more than 2 nodes of odd degree.
The degree of a node refers to the number of edges connected to it. For an Eulerian circuit (a path that visits every edge exactly once and returns to the starting point) to exist, all nodes must have an even degree. This is because if you enter a node via one edge, another edge must be available to exit the node. Pairs of edges must be present to allow for this entry and exit via different routes. Since the Königsberg graph had four nodes with an odd degree, such a path was impossible. This insight marked the beginning of graph theory and demonstrated the utility of modeling patterns of relationships in terms of a graph as a powerful analytical model. Euler's work marked the inception of using graphs to model relationships and understand interconnected features within various problem domains. This laid the groundwork for the field of graph theory, which continues to provide valuable tools for analyzing complex networks.
More than 100 years later, Arthur Cayley conceived a category of graph known as a tree, a term that remains conventional today. He defined a tree as a graph structure in which there is only one possible path between any two nodes. This means the graph is acyclic (not looping) and that breaking any edge separates the graph into two components. In other words, breaking an edge in a tree results in two separate, disconnected subgraphs.
As electronics and computing emerged and infrastructure planning scaled, graphs became increasingly useful. Networks of wiring and grids of connectivity naturally utilized a graph interpretation, which became integral to societal engineering. This ushered in greater utility in discovering features that can be discerned or relied upon based on graph properties.
Algorithms
As the utility of modeling our world in terms of nodes and connections expanded, so too did the need to measure the qualities of these graphs and develop methods for exploring their structures. Let's consider how we could methodically discover a path between two nodes. With sufficient computation, we can ensure we find a route, assuming a connected path exists. We can use either depth-first search (DFS) or breadth-first search (BFS). Each method is sufficient, given enough computational power, to explore the connections from a starting node to any other specified node.
In depth-first search, you travel down a path of branches as far as possible until you hit a dead end. You then backtrack to a previous fork and try a different path. Eventually, you explore all possible paths, and as long as a path between node A and node B exists, this method will find it.
Similarly, breadth-first search is equally capable. It is another brute force method that exhaustively explores all possibilities until a path is uncovered. Instead of diving deep into one path, BFS explores all neighbors of the starting node first, then moves to the next level of nodes, and so on. This systematic approach ensures that if a path exists, it will be found.
While DFS and BFS are sufficient for finding a path between two nodes and can reveal paths across a graph, they are not computationally efficient for finding the shortest path between A and B. These methods also uncover paths between A and all other nodes that come before B in the queue. Relating this to using language to communicate directions through a graph of concepts, it would be extremely inefficient if, for every idea you wished to communicate, you had to navigate through every other concept you know until you stumbled upon the one you meant to highlight.
Clearly, there is an ability to direct communication towards the intended idea without backtracking or testing every possible connection at each step of abstraction. This raises the question of what methods exist to reliably direct us through a graph. How can we compute an efficient path between two specific nodes?
Directed Navigation Algorithms

When you enter a destination in a GPS mapping service like Google Maps, how does it determine the series of roads you need to travel to get from your starting location to your desired destination in just seconds? Think of a series of roads as a graph of connections: road 1 connects to road 2, which splits into other roads going in various directions, continuing to branch out. Our road infrastructure is a network, where each intersection is a node and the roads between are edges.
If the system used methods like depth-first search (DFS) or breadth-first search (BFS) to find a route, it would be extremely inefficient. These methods would systematically test every possible road to see if it eventually leads to the destination. While they can find a path, they don't guarantee it will be the shortest or most efficient one. For instance, a DFS might stumble upon a possible route early on, but without exploring further, it might miss a shorter path that significantly reduces travel time.
This inefficiency is impractical for applications like GPS, where quick and optimal route planning is crucial. Therefore, more sophisticated algorithms are needed to reliably and efficiently find the best path between two points. Let's investigate the methods developed to solve this problem effectively.
The genesis of directed navigation algorithms began with Edsger W. Dijkstra, a Dutch-born computer scientist and mathematician. In 1956, over two centuries after Euler's bridge problem laid the foundations of graph theory, Dijkstra developed a method for discovering the shortest path between any two nodes in a weighted graph.
Weights in a graph refer to values associated with edges, which can represent various metrics such as distance, time, cost, or any other measure relevant to the problem at hand. In the context of GPS navigation, for example, the weights typically represent the distance between nodes (intersections).
Dijkstra's algorithm effectively finds the shortest path between two nodes in a weighted graph, where each edge carries a cost. The algorithm initializes by setting the cost from the source node to zero and to infinity for all other nodes. It then systematically explores all accessible nodes, updating the shortest known cost to each node as it progresses. This ensures that, upon completion, the algorithm has determined the most efficient route to each node from the source.
A*
Since Dijkstra's breakthrough, significant progress has been made in finding shortest paths through networks, most notably with the development of the A* algorithm. An array of variants and subtle iterations have been developed to address various specific needs. The * (star) notation denotes that the method achieves optimization. Colloquially, the star indicates that the algorithm guarantees finding the shortest path, although the exact implications of this symbol can vary between algorithms. While this notation is notably consistent within the context of search algorithms, its meaning may diverge as the context shifts.
It is also notable that receiving the * notation often relies on satisfying a particular condition. For A*, this condition is that the heuristic used must be "admissible," meaning it does not overestimate the actual cost to reach the goal. This style of condition for optimality is common across algorithms; for instance, another algorithm might rely on a certain amount of memory being available. If the condition is met, the algorithm is guaranteed to find the shortest path.
So, what is notable about A*? This algorithm is significant because it informs us about what a reliable way of navigating a graph looks like and what components and pieces of information need to be known. Although the exact specifics of this algorithm may not directly mirror what the human mind does or what language communicates, it is still worth investigating its mechanics to see what insights we can apply to understanding language.
Essence of A*
A* is a process of exploring a graph from a starting node to available neighbors using a priority queue that incorporates a measure of meta information (information collected from outside the immediate perspective of the graph). This piece of information is referred to as a heuristic, which provides a guidance estimate towards solving the problem. Generally, heuristics help find solutions more quickly, but they do not necessarily guarantee an optimal solution.
In the specific case of A*, the heuristic combined with the algorithmic process ensures optimality if it meets the condition of being admissible. This means that the heuristic does not overestimate the actual cost to reach the goal. A* typically uses heuristics such as the straight-line distance (Euclidean distance) or Manhattan distance between nodes. Important to note however, these are just examples of heuristics, and the algorithm is not intrinsically tied to them. The heuristic can vary depending on the problem space being navigated, and different cost measures can be applied as appropriate for different problems. When an admissible heuristic is used, A* guarantees that the shortest path will be found.
A* tracks the heuristic for both the distance or cost from the starting node to an available neighboring node and from the neighboring node to the target node. It combines these measures to create a score, called f(n), which is the sum of the actual cost to reach the node (g(n)) and the heuristic estimated cost to reach the goal (h(n)). The node with the lowest f(n) value has the highest priority to be explored and will be assessed first.
As each node is explored, the algorithm calculates the costs for all adjacent nodes, updating their g, h, and f values accordingly. A* will always explore the path with the lowest f value, ensuring that it prioritizes the most promising paths first. If a path's f value becomes higher than others, the algorithm will switch to explore paths with lower f values, always prioritizing the lowest value to search next based on the current map of learned f values. Nodes that have been fully explored, meaning their neighbors' costs have been assessed, are marked as closed and will not be reconsidered.
By continually selecting path directions based on the lowest f value, A* guarantees that the shortest path from the origin to the target is found, provided the heuristic is admissible.
Why must language be doing something different?
If we view the problem as discovering a method to reliably find paths through our graph of concepts to communicate information about, we can presume that there may be similar mechanics at play as the search algorithms we have developed for physical navigation. However, there are key differences related to A* that suggest it may not be the exact solution the mind uses to establish these paths between concepts. Despite potential overlaps, it appears we need to establish a new mechanical process for conceptual pathfinding.
What details seem to be inconsistent with how we mentally formulate a path? Let's identify the distinctions between how A* operates and how the mind must be operating, in hopes that this can provide the right constraints to achieve a framework that leads or forces us into the solution.
First, A* relies on a heuristic like the physical Manhattan distance. In the mind, there is unlikely to be a method for practically achieving this measure. Concepts are not separated by physical distances but rather by patterns of relationships. This highlights that the issue lies with the heuristic choice rather than with the algorithm itself.
The essence of A*—assessing an approximate cost measure at every visited incremental location as to the combined cost to reach that particular location and from that location to the target—could still be a sound method for discerning a path in more abstract constructs, such as a graph of concepts. In this context, the cost likely reduces to an energy expense to “travel,” contemplate, or consider a particular node. The cost could also relate to language itself, measuring how many lexical resources are required to go from each incremental step.
Either way, the point is that there is merit in the algorithm’s logic, but the heuristic itself may need to be adjusted to more accurately resemble the notion that our minds use to measure cost.
Secondly, the A* algorithm is primarily concerned with finding the shortest path between two nodes. However, in language, the shortest path is often not the most needed or effective. We seem capable of dynamically adjusting our paths based on the context and purpose of the communication. Sometimes, we may need to take a scenic route, rigorously laying out the precise steps that lead from one idea to another. Other times, we might need to zigzag to detect how particular conceptual forms are understood by the recipient, ensuring the intended nuance is expressed.
The mind appears to have the agency to meander across various possible paths, guided by an instinctive intuition about the existence of a path between a given point and the target. Without being able to precisely discern the range of pathing possibilities, it seems that the shortest path is not necessarily the most optimal in all cases. This observation makes me question whether the rigid optimization focus of A*-adjacent algorithms are truly at the root of the mental process for navigating conceptual graphs.
What are the important parts about A* that likely plays a role in the navigational algorithm of language? The biggest revelation that I intuit about A* is that weighting edges with a notion of cost can effectively reduce the resources needed to find an optimal path. There likely is a cost measure implemented at some resolution of the entire process of transferring from one idea to the next.
For a living system, the most obvious costly resource is energy. Thinking is expensive in terms of biological systems' energy supply. According to the paper "An Energy Budget for Signaling in the Grey Matter of the Brain" by Attwell and Laughlin (2001), the brain consumes about 20% of the body's total energy despite only making up about 2% of its mass. This suggests that the notion of cost in mental processes may reduce to energy consumption or a proxy for it.
Regardless, the relevant measure of fundamental cost for our search algorithm will be the framing in which the shortest path is revealed. In communication, the search space is not an individual conceptual graph but rather the central zone of a Venn diagram of all the graphs for the n number of parties communicating. This search often requires iteratively testing the boundaries of this overlapping region to discern what paths exist.
Once a path is found and boundaries of overlap are established, there can be refinement in pathing that progresses more directly from origin to target. Lexical jargon can then be introduced to represent this path, allowing a path to be invoked simply by stating a word. For example, in the mortal man argument, the term "mortal man" is enough to reform the pathing without needing to state the entire logic every time: All men are mortal, Joe is a man, Joe is mortal. A=B, B=C, C=A.
We can reduce the cost of travel when the central overlap Venn diagram space is largest and jargon is jointly agreed and understood. As paths of communication take place and are discovered, each additional traversal of the path can refine the travel distance (cost expense), likely with a process akin to Q-learning. The descriptions you give most often and have to repeat regularly trend towards optimal explanations, transitioning from a navigation problem to a memory learning problem, as you recount the optimal path you have already found.
Precisely understanding the algorithmic nature behind our cognitive processes is unlikely to be fully described or deduced by philosophical means alone; experimental methods will likely be needed to uncover these details. One such experimental method could involve testing the semantic understanding similarity between differing lexical paths.
This brings us to the concept of semantic vectors, a method used in natural language processing (NLP) to embed words in a high-dimensional space based on their contextual usage. In NLP, context is often defined by the proximity of words to each other in a string of text, meaning that words appearing together frequently are considered contextually related. However, this notion of context differs from the conceptual framework I propose, where context is defined by the degree to which a concept is fundamental in a lineage to another concept.

The Notion of Cost in Language: A Hypothesis Inspired by Mandelbrot
Mandelbrot's generalization of Zipf's Law introduced the concept of a "cost" associated with word frequency, captured by the parameter β\betaβ. This idea suggests that the frequency of a word could be inversely related to the length of the linguistic "tower" needed to support its use. In other words, less frequent words might incur a higher "cost" in terms of the additional language required to establish their semantic completeness. This interpretation of Mandelbrot's cost parameter offers a way to understand the interplay between word frequency and the complexity of meaning, providing a possible explanation for why certain words are used less frequently—they simply require more linguistic effort to convey their full significance.
