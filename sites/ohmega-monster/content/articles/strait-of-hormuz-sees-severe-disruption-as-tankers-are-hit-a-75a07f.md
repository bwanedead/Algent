The Strait of Hormuz is a chokepoint: a narrow route whose disruption can ripple far beyond the water itself because so much oil and gas trade depends on it.

That matters here because the evidence points to a strait under severe conflict-driven disruption, not a cleanly verified closure. Reuters reporting in the profile says vessel transits fell to multi-week lows, while JMIC/UKMTO, the maritime advisory system ship operators use, said commercial traffic continued at reduced levels and that the southern route remained available. JMIC/UKMTO also kept the maritime security threat level at SEVERE.

The danger is not rhetorical. Two tankers were struck in the strait area, and one crew member was killed, with additional injuries reported. JMIC/UKMTO later described further incidents, including the MOMBASA B and AL BAHYAH, one of which left one person dead and another injured. Those are the facts that explain why traffic dropped.

They also explain the political fight over what “open” means. Iran’s IRGC navy said the strait was closed until further notice, while U.S. officials said the waterway remains open and that Iran does not control it. Those are competing claims, not settled operational fact.

CENTCOM said its strikes were aimed at degrading Iran’s ability to continue attacking international shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. In its July 12 release, the command said it hit Iranian air-defense systems, coastal radar sites, missile and drone capabilities, and small boats. That is the stated intent; it is not, by itself, proof the threat has been removed or that ships are being physically blocked rather than avoiding the area.

A strong alternative reading is that the drop in traffic reflects avoidance under threat rather than an enforced blockade. TradeWinds reported a U.S. strike on a VLCC that was trying to breach Washington’s renewed blockade of Iran’s ports, and shipping-industry voices quoted elsewhere in the profile argued that crews are staying away because the corridor is too dangerous. That view fits the traffic data, but it still does not prove a physical or legal shutdown.

So the answer is narrower than either side’s slogans: Hormuz is severely disrupted, ships are avoiding it, and violence is real. A full blockade is not yet independently established in this profile.
