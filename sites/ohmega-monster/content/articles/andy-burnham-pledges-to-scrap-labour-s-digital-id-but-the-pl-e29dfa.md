Andy Burnham says he will scrap Labour’s digital ID scheme and put the time and resource into the cost of living instead. But the move is not a clean kill of an intact plan. By the time he made the pledge, the government had already dropped the mandatory element of the scheme after backlash and relaunching it as voluntary digital infrastructure.

That chronology matters. When Sir Keir Starmer first announced the plan in September 2025, it was meant to be mandatory for right-to-work checks — employer checks on whether someone is legally allowed to work — and tied to illegal immigration enforcement. By January, ministers were saying it was free and voluntary for individuals, while the digital right-to-work side would still be mandatory for employers by the end of the parliament. In March, the government consultation on digital identity said people would have no legal obligation to carry or present the ID and access to public services would not depend on it. So Burnham is reversing a weakened policy, not abolishing an untouched one.

The original political logic was enforcement first, convenience second. The government argued the scheme would help clamp down on illegal work and, later, make public services easier to access. But the policy ran into the sort of objections that tend to sink identity schemes: privacy, surveillance, exclusion and the risk that a system built for one purpose spreads into others. Nearly 3 million people signed a petition against it, and the Commons Library says public and parliamentary pressure helped push the scheme backward before Burnham moved.

Burnham’s office is also trying to make the case as a governing-priorities story. The claim is that the money and attention earmarked for digital ID should go to everyday pressures such as living costs. That is a plausible political argument, but the savings are not cleanly proven. The OBR, the independent Office for Budget Responsibility, estimated the programme would cost £1.8 billion over three years, but the government rejected that figure, and Parliament’s own library said the final cost depended on design choices. Burnham’s allies say the money is “not an insignificant amount”; that is a political rationale, not a verified bottom line.

The broader significance is that this is as much about what Labour wants to spend its political capital on as it is about digital technology. Burnham is signaling that the next government would rather talk about bills, services and everyday economic pressure than rebuild a contested national identity system. Conservatives argue he is claiming credit for a retreat that was already under way. That criticism is hard to dismiss. But it does not change the basic fact that the policy was already weakened, and Burnham is now trying to take it the rest of the way off the table.
