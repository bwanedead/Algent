On 21 September 2026, World Peace Day, Turkey's culture and tourism minister Mehmet Nuri Ersoy said a clay fragment found on the royal citadel of Hattusa had been identified after about a year of study as a new copy of the Kadesh Peace Treaty, the 13th-century BC agreement between the Hittite Empire and Egypt that is the oldest surviving written peace treaty. The piece is said to preserve the section on people who fled from one kingdom to the other, including wording that their homes not be destroyed. No Akkadian transcription, specialist name or script analysis has been published, so whether that wording is new or duplicates known lines cannot yet be judged.

Hattusa, near the modern village of Boğazkale in central Turkey's Çorum Province, was the Hittite capital from about 1650 BC. Its palace and temple archives held about 25,000 clay tablets in cuneiform — wedge-shaped writing pressed into clay — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and Memory of the World archive.

The new piece came from Büyükkale, the fortified royal citadel at the city's heart, near a structure called Building E. Only one face survives; the rest is broken away. That palace context fits the pattern: Hattusa's diplomatic copies were stored there. If confirmed, it would join Akkadian copies first excavated at Hattusa in 1906-1912 and now dispersed among museums in Istanbul, Ankara, Berlin and St Petersburg after the early practice of dividing finds between excavators and host country. Excavations at Büyükkale, currently led by the Istanbul branch of the German Archaeological Institute under Prof. Andreas Schachner, are continuing in hopes of finding joining pieces. No dimensions, condition report, conservation location or display plan has been announced.

How it was identified is also narrow. The clay was not scientifically dated. The treaty is dated historically to about 1259 BC, some 15 years after the Battle of Kadesh around 1274 BC, by Hittite-Egyptian chronology. According to the ministry, attribution rests on language — Akkadian, the diplomatic lingua franca of the Late Bronze Age Near East — plus handwriting style and overlap with known manuscripts. The ministry described this only as "bilimsel incelemelerle belirlendi" — determined by scientific examinations — thanking the excavation team and scientists without naming an Assyriologist or publishing the analysis. No peer-reviewed publication or institute bulletin on the fragment has appeared. Until a transcription is published, the identification remains a ministry claim, not an independently authenticated reading. The ministry said the fragment had undergone about a year of detailed examination before the 21 September unveiling — a communications choice, not a scientific date.

## Forced return, not refuge

What the fragment is said to preserve is Articles 12-20, the section regulating people who fled the lands of Egypt and Hatti. In the ministry summary, readable lines refer to people fleeing and include that their homes not be destroyed.

In the treaty as previously known, those articles imposed a reciprocal obligation: each king must return fugitives to the other side, and neither side may punish or harm the returned persons and their families. The newly highlighted home-protection wording sits inside that framework. It is unusually humane for the Late Bronze Age in explicitly protecting persons and property after return, but it is a Bronze Age interstate control mechanism, not a right to remain.

That distinction is the core dispute. Scholarship summarized by Arkeonews on the same material cautions: "These were diplomatic obligations negotiated between two ancient rulers, rather than asylum protections comparable to modern refugee law." The clause protects people *after* forced return; it does not protect them *from* return. Modern refugee law centres on not sending people back to danger and on an individual's right to seek asylum — ideas with no counterpart in the Kadesh framework, which has no notion of double criminality or a political-offence exception.

The inversion is visible in how the announcement was heard. SyriacPress headlined the same fragment as containing "provisions on the treatment of asylum seekers," describing "individuals seeking asylum" — a natural modern gloss on people who fled, but one that installs a legal status the treaty does not grant. Arkeonews, working from the same ministry material, warned against that reading. The ministry's own emphasis — detaching the home-protection line from the forced-return obligation and presenting it as timeless humanitarian protection — makes the presentist reading easy to reproduce. The full Akkadian phrasing has not been released, so it is not possible to say whether the "homes not be destroyed" wording adds anything new or duplicates lines already known.

## From battlefield to parity — and a World Peace Day stage

The Kadesh treaty ended decades of rivalry over Syria after the Battle of Kadesh, an indecisive clash around 1274 BC between Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II and Hittite king Muwatalli II, predecessor of Hattusili III. About fifteen years later Hattusili III and Ramesses II concluded a parity treaty — unusual for its time in addressing the two kings as equals and pledging "good peace and good fraternity forever."

Beyond ending war, it set mutual non-aggression, mutual assistance against third parties, and the extradition provisions now in focus. Originals were inscribed on silver tablets, now lost. What survives is both sides' copies: Egyptian hieroglyphs carved on temple walls at Karnak and the Ramesseum, and Hittite copies in Akkadian cuneiform on clay at Hattusa. That dual survival makes it the only Late Bronze Age treaty with both parties' versions extant, and the reason an enlarged copy is displayed at United Nations Headquarters in New York as a symbol of diplomacy. The new fragment does not rewrite that framework. At most it enriches one section — a rare Bronze Age example of interstate law regulating individuals, not just rulers — and shows the Hattusa archive is still incomplete.

The International Day of Peace, observed by the United Nations on 21 September as World Peace Day, provided the stage. Ersoy said the statements concerning destruction of homes "carry a powerful message from thousands of years ago to the present," presenting the home-protection wording as a message about protecting human life and dignity and linking it explicitly to hope that Palestinians can live in safety.

Taken on its own terms, the ministerial reading steelmans to this: Anatolia has been a land of peace for millennia, and even Bronze Age great powers recognised that homes and families should not be destroyed when people were sent back — a humane element worth recalling when heritage is quoted for present diplomacy.

The scholarly counter-reading steelmans differently: the humane element is real, but it cannot be detached from forced return. The treaty obliges return; the protection applies after. Presenting home-protection as if it were early asylum misdescribes the mechanism and obscures that the treaty offers no right to remain.

A third reading, from heritage-diplomacy analysts, notes pattern rather than motive. Researchers have documented how Turkish archaeology is often framed with superlatives — "oldest," "first," centring Anatolia — and how cultural heritage has become a political resource. In that light, analysts argue, a year-long study unveiled on World Peace Day with foreign-policy messaging should be read also as state soft-power communication, not purely scholarly publication. That is a plausible analogy drawn from reporting on other heritage rollouts, not direct evidence that this date was chosen for soft power, and no independent response from the German Archaeological Institute or Egyptian antiquities authorities to the Articles 12-20 reading or the Palestine-linked framing has appeared to test it.

What remains open is philological, not political: the exact Akkadian text, whether it joins an existing tablet in Istanbul, Ankara, Berlin or St Petersburg or represents a separate copy, where it will be conserved and when it will be accessible. Until the transcription and criteria are published, the fragment is best held as a promising archival addition whose most quoted line — homes not destroyed — is genuine in its humane impulse and limited in its legal meaning: protection after return, not protection from return.
