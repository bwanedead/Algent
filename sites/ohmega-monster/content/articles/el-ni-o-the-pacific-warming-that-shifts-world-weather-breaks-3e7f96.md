The central Pacific was 3.11C warmer than normal on 21 September, beating the daily high of 3.08C set in November 2015. The reading comes from El Nino's benchmark box in the central-eastern equatorial Pacific, and forecasts point higher — toward a monthly peak that would dwarf any event in 150 years, if it verifies at a size no system has been tested against.

That box is the Nino 3.4 region, a rectangle straddling the equator (170W-120W, 5S-5N). El Nino — the periodic warming there that shifts weather worldwide — is judged by how warm that box is, and by which ruler you use to strip out the background warming of the oceans. The choice decides whether this is already the strongest on record and what its heat means for global temperature and for drought, flood and extreme heat.

## A daily record, not yet a monthly one

The 3.11C is a daily Nino 3.4 anomaly calculated the way NOAA's Oceanic Nino Index (ONI) does it — sea-surface temperature minus a centred 30-year average — applied day-by-day to NOAA's OISSTv2.1 sea-surface dataset by Carbon Brief, the UK climate news site.

That is not the operational ONI, which is a three-month rolling mean, and it is not the monthly record. August averaged about 2.45C for the month. That already beats every El Nino except 2015-16 and the sparse reconstruction of 1877-78, but remains below those peaks.

The timing matters. El Nino typically peaks in November or December, and every strong event on record kept strengthening after mid-September. At 3.11C daily and 2.45C monthly, this one is already well into very strong territory.

## Why two rulers give two answers

Whether 2026 is the "strongest on record" depends on how you remove the warming of the oceans themselves.

ONI subtracts the latest centred 30-year average in the Nino box. In a warming world that leaves a bias: it tends to inflate El Nino and understate La Nina, because the whole tropical ocean is warmer than 30 years ago.

The Relative ONI (RONI), adopted by the US Climate Prediction Center in February 2026, tries to isolate the El Nino-driven part. It subtracts the average anomaly across the entire tropical ocean between 20N and 20S, not just the 30-year mean in the box. NOAA says it matches observed impacts like drought better, though some researchers argue it may understate warming-driven impacts. The debate is active.

On that second ruler the picture shifts. Daily RONI stood around 2.5C in mid-September, a record for the time of year but still about 0.7C below the all-time daily RONI peak in late December 1982. The 2023-24 El Nino shows why it matters: it peaked around 2.0-2.1C on ONI but only about 1.5C on RONI, borderline strong.

Neither ruler is wrong. ONI is the long-standing standard for ranking events back to 1950 and, with reconstructions, to 1877. RONI is what the US centre now uses to judge strength and forecast impacts, because it gives a cleaner read on how much warmth is actually driving the atmosphere. On ONI, 2026 has already broken the daily record and is closing on the monthly one. On RONI, it is record-strong for September but not yet all-time.

## How it got hot — and how high forecasts point

The World Meteorological Organization and the US Climate Prediction Center point to the same fuel: exceptionally warm water at the surface and even warmer water below, plus an atmosphere that has locked in.

Weekly Nino 3.4 values rose from about 1.5C in May-July to 2.2-2.6C by mid-August. Below the surface, temperatures were more than 8C above average in July and early August, with even larger anomalies at depth, according to secondary summaries of the agencies' diagnostics.

That heat reservoir fuels feedback that reinforces warming. A developing positive Indian Ocean Dipole — a periodic warming in the western Indian Ocean that can reinforce El Nino — forecast around 0.9C for September-November, is adding to the loop. The Nino 3.4 index crossed 0.5C in April, hit 1.6C in June and shot above 2C in early July, outpacing any prior event's development. No peer-reviewed study yet quantifies how much human-caused warming contributed to the 3.11C versus natural variability.

What makes this more than a daily record is the unanimity and margin of the forecast for the monthly peak in November to January. Carbon Brief's analysis of 14 modelling groups (674 runs) puts the median monthly ONI peak around 4.1C, with 80% of runs between 3.4C and 4.6C. Every run peaks above the 2015-16 monthly record of 2.75C.

On RONI the same ensemble still points to a record, but less unanimously: median around 3.1C versus the 1982-83 RONI record of 2.69C, with 77% of runs breaking it. The Climate Prediction Center's outlook mirrors that strength: for October-December it gives a 98% chance of very strong (above 2C on RONI) and a 75% chance of historic strength above 2.5C, a level that would exceed every event since 1950.

No seasonal system has been verified against an event this large, because none has occurred, and models ran warm this summer — August came in about 0.3C below forecasts. All strong El Ninos on record did keep strengthening after mid-September, which supports further intensification, but the exact peak and whether RONI also falls remain open.

## Why 2027 carries the heat

El Nino-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the largest year-to-year driver of global mean temperature, but its effect lags the ocean by months. Heat stored in the Pacific leaks into the atmosphere after the ocean peaks, so the warmest year typically follows the El Nino winter.

That lag is why 2026 and 2027 look different. The first six months of 2026 were the third-warmest January-June on record, about 1.4C above pre-industrial levels, behind only 2024 and 2025. Carbon Brief's central estimate still has 2026 at about 1.51C, with a 35% chance of beating 2024 as the warmest year — up from 19% in April — but whether it sets a record will depend on the dataset.

For 2027 the signal is clearer. Carbon Brief projects around 1.7C above pre-industrial, which would comfortably set a new warmest-year record, because the ocean peak in late 2026 would be felt most the following year. The WMO's September-November outlook already favours above-normal temperatures across almost all land areas.

A very strong El Nino tilts the odds for rainfall and temperature far from the Pacific — what scientists call teleconnections — but strength alone does not determine severity in any single country. Other basins modulate the signal.

For September-November and into the boreal winter, the classic risk pattern flagged by WMO and the inter-agency Global ENSO Analysis Cell includes drought across several tropical regions and floods in parts of South America, plus extreme heat and wildfires elsewhere. The concurrent Dipole and warmer tropical Atlantic may alter those links in different regions.

The humanitarian exposure is framed as probability, not observed disaster. Both the estimate that up to 50 million people could be affected and the projection of about 451,000 excess deaths by February 2027 are model-based projections reported via ReliefWeb, not observed counts, and carry wide uncertainty.

Forecasters put near-certain odds on El Nino persisting through the northern winter — WMO near 100% through February 2027 and the US centre at 100% through December-February — with no dynamical signal for a flip to La Nina before spring. In the longer term history points the other way as precedent, not forecast: all five super El Ninos since 1970 collapsed quickly to neutral or La Nina after their peaks, a pattern cited as a reason to watch for a swift transition in mid-2027.

What remains open is whether the monthly ONI record falls and by how much, whether RONI also breaks its 1982 highs, how the Dipole and warm Atlantic reshape the regional odds, and how much of the 3.11C reflects human-caused warming versus natural variability — a question for which no peer-reviewed attribution study yet exists.
