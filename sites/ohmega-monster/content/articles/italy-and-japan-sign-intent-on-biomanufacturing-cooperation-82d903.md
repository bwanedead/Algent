Italy and Japan have signed a dichiarazione di intenti to strengthen scientific cooperation, open new collaborations and develop joint initiatives, according to a press note from Italy’s National Research Council, the CNR.

The declaration is a starting signal for joint biomanufacturing research and pilot-style collaboration, not a funded treaty. In practical terms, it is about turning biomass and waste into useful products through biological processes, and about beginning a joint pipeline for proposals and cooperation.

The Italian note says the deal names biomanufacturing as a strategic priority. It identifies the signatories as Marco Mancini, a senior official at Italy’s university and research ministry, and Yasuyoshi Kakita, a vice minister at Japan’s education, science and technology ministry.

The substance is narrower and more concrete than a generic “science partnership.” The CNR says the intent opens the way to joint research and innovation projects in bioproduction, using microorganisms and enzymes to turn organic waste and biomass into higher-value products such as materials, molecules, active ingredients, bioplastics, biopolymers, biodegradable plastics and biofuels.

In plain terms, biomanufacturing means using biological systems or processes to make useful products, rather than relying only on conventional industrial chemistry. Both sides want that because it can turn waste and biomass into higher-value materials and chemicals.

The available record does not establish the legal or financial architecture behind the declaration. The CNR note does not set out a dedicated budget, timetable or annexed implementation plan, and the underlying signed text was not available in this run.

There is, however, broader bilateral context. Japan’s foreign ministry says the Japan-Italy Action Plan was launched at the G7 summit in Apulia in June 2024, and later official Japanese language in a 2026 joint statement describes the two countries’ relationship as a “Special Strategic Partnership” under a 2024-2027 Action Plan. That joint statement says the two leaders wanted to deepen scientific and technological cooperation in advanced areas including AI robotics, semiconductors and biomanufacturing, alongside industrial partnerships, investment and trade.

So the relationship has two layers: a specific Italy-Japan intent centered on biomanufacturing and bioproduction, inside a wider diplomatic relationship that already had an action-plan framework and later broadened official language. What the record does not yet show is a verified all-sector research program, a binding treaty, or the funding and execution machinery that would turn the intent into a full bilateral scheme.

The phrase “Rome-Tokyo axis” is best treated as a geopolitical gloss, not as a document-backed label for a single integrated program.
