Oil is still near $100 because the world's most important oil waterway is still mostly closed, even as American and Iranian envoys resumed indirect talks in New York.

On Sept. 22-23, the two sides shuttled messages through Qatari mediators to try for a short framework to restart negotiations. There is no meeting planned between U.S. President Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, and the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow passage between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula that carries about a fifth of the world's oil — remains mostly shut.

## Why this strait sets the price

Hormuz is the chokepoint that sets the global oil price. In 2018, the last full baseline published by the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the agency that tracks global energy flows, it averaged 21 million barrels a day, about 21% of world petroleum use.

There is almost no way around it. Only Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates have pipelines that bypass Hormuz, with about 3.8 million barrels a day of spare capacity at the end of 2018 — not a substitute for 21 million. Most crude and condensate through Hormuz went to Asian markets. A second chokepoint for Gulf oil toward the Suez Canal, the Bab el-Mandeb Strait off Yemen, carried several million barrels a day in 2018; closing either forces long detours around Africa.

## A shuttle, not a summit

The channel is shuttle diplomacy, where mediators carry messages between sides that do not meet face-to-face. Qatar is leading it, with Pakistan named alongside it and Oman as the planned venue.

U.S. envoy Steve Witkoff said in a post on X that the United States and Iran held lengthy talks Sept. 22 via mediators who shuttled between them all day and would continue work. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi met Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani on the sidelines Sept. 22, according to Qatar's state news agency. Trump said Sept. 22-23 that Witkoff and Jared Kushner — the former Trump adviser acting as envoy — had a "very productive meeting" with mediators and that there is "a lot of momentum" for a deal.

The aim, as Qatari Foreign Ministry spokesman Majed al-Ansari framed it, is a starter framework to restart talks and pave the way for broader regional dialogue, not a final settlement. An interim agreement in June that would have eased financial sanctions and unfrozen Iranian assets in return for talks on limiting Iran's nuclear program unravelled within weeks over the same issue now blocking progress: shipping rules and fees in Hormuz.

Both leaders are in New York for the United Nations General Assembly, the annual gathering of world leaders, but no bilateral is scheduled. Ansari said reopening Hormuz and a viable regional ceasefire are prerequisites for durable dialogue.

A preparatory meeting planned for Sept. 14 in Oman was cancelled after a drone attack forced Saudi Aramco, the Saudi state oil company, to shut its East-West pipeline on Sept. 11. Saudi Arabia blamed an Iraqi militia. The line restarted Sept. 22 at a low rate, with full restart expected in six to eight weeks.

Gulf states say they need a common position before meeting Iran and met on the UNGA margins Sept. 21. Underneath is a trust deficit. Ansari described both sides as expecting the other to blink first, and public positions have hardened. Trump on Sept. 22 framed the choice as deal versus "annihilate the Islamic Republic," while Pezeshkian told the General Assembly Sept. 23 that Iran will not surrender but believes in diplomacy, saying "It cannot be the case that everyone benefits from the Strait of Hormuz while we are denied access." The U.S. delegation walked out at the start of that speech.

No joint communique of the shuttle, Qatari Foreign Ministry statement, transcript of Ansari's remarks, Omani notice about the Sept. 14 meeting, or U.S. State Department or Iranian Foreign Ministry readout has been published, and no Aramco press release confirming the pipeline shutdown has appeared. The account so far rests on the Witkoff post, Trump remarks, an Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesperson and Qatari state media as reported by Reuters. That does not make the shuttle false, but it keeps it one step short of official confirmation.

## Three demands for one waterway

"Reopening Hormuz" sounds technical. The politics are why flows stay low.

Iran wants reciprocal access and retained control. Tehran says the strait cannot benefit everyone while Iran is denied access under blockade and sanctions. Conditions conveyed via state media for reopening within seven days include immediate lifting of the U.S. naval blockade, immediate release of frozen Iranian assets, and an end to war across all "resistance" fronts. Iran seeks to keep a degree of control over traffic, possibly with Oman and neighbors, to preserve leverage after the June deal collapsed over fees. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said Sept. 22 that U.S. blaming of Iran for fuel price hikes tries to evade responsibility for military aggression that disrupted shipping.

The United States, and U.S. Central Command which oversees American forces in the Middle East, wants free passage enforced by pressure. Washington says pre-war freedom of navigation must be restored. Central Command destroyed tankers linked to Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps in early September after IRGC ballistic missile attacks on U.S. warships, describing the vessels as a shadow network funding the IRGC and its proxies. The broader posture is described as sanctions, naval blockade and limited airstrikes, not invasion preparation.

Gulf and Qatari mediators want a common security framework. Qatar put on the table at the Trump-Gulf meeting a proposal for a framework involving Iran and Arab Gulf states with guarantees and economic interdependence, which Iran is reviewing. Gulf states are wary of giving Iran too much power over Hormuz while hosting U.S. bases that have come under Iranian attack. Qatar simultaneously condemned Houthi drone and missile attacks on Saudi Arabia, saying Saudi security is integral to Gulf security.

That three-way structure is why a starter framework alone does not equal reopening. Even if mediators find language to restart talks, the contradiction between control as leverage and free passage as principle remains.

## What oil is pricing — and what would have to change

Brent crude, the global benchmark, traded around $101 a barrel in Asian trading Sept. 22, near the low end of its range since Sept. 10, then fell toward $97 to $100 on Sept. 22-23 on the pipeline restart and talk hopes. On Sept. 23 it hovered around $100.

The EIA's September outlook, finalized Sept. 3, still has Brent averaging about $90 in the second half of 2026 before falling to $74 in 2027 as inventories rebuild — implying about $16 of elevation from today. The EIA attributes that to two things together: global oil inventories down 400 million barrels so far in 2026, and Middle East export constraints via Hormuz plus limits on alternative routes. It does not break out how much is Iran or Hormuz alone.

What is established downstream is tightness: the EIA expects U.S. diesel and heating oil inventories below the five-year low through much of 2027, with elevated refiner margins if Hormuz traffic normalizes soon — and higher for longer if constraints persist. No decomposition model was provided to support a widely cited $5 to $10 per barrel Iran/Hormuz risk premium, so the Iran-specific share remains unquantified.

Traders are pricing off observed tanker tracking, not official declarations, and official claims diverge. Central Command's top commander in the Middle East, Admiral Brad Cooper, said Sept. 19 that U.S. efforts enabled export of 1 billion barrels through Hormuz over the prior two months — roughly 16 million barrels a day — far above independent analyst estimates and prior U.S. official statements, raising credibility questions. The more consistent description is that traffic remains a fraction of pre-war levels with slow recovery despite U.S. naval efforts amid continued attacks on shipping.

The EIA assumes Middle East production rises gradually on increasing Hormuz flows and bypass pipelines, but constraints persist through end-2026. Normalization by Dec. 31 would need three interlocking triggers, none yet in sight: a ceasefire and security guarantees for shipping, which Ansari calls a prerequisite while attacks continue; a political formula bridging retained control versus free passage; and physical infrastructure security, as the East-West pipeline attack showed how spillover can derail diplomacy and bypass options at once. Markets will not take a communique as proof; sustained tanker data back toward pre-war levels would be needed.

The skeptical case is that the shuttle is positioning, not breakthrough. Witkoff's "lengthy talks" and Trump's "momentum" sit against hardliner backlash: the IRGC-associated Tasnim outlet said Araqchi was wrong to contact Witkoff and Kushner as a "major gift to Trump," Iranian sources said the delegation was ordered not to meet face-to-face by the Supreme National Security Council, and some hardliners viewed the New York trip as an insult to slain Ali Khamenei. Trump himself suggested the situation could end after the Nov. 3 U.S. midterms. Whether Gulf states pursue direct Iran engagement in parallel to the U.S.-led shuttle, or instead of it, is also unresolved.

For now, the hinge holds: talks have resumed, the strait has not.
