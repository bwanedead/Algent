A database of more than 16,000 megaprojects — builds costing over $1 billion — finds about nine in ten go over budget and only about 0.5% hit budget, time and promised benefits together. About 8.5% manage the first two. That baseline is a portable test for any glossy render: ask what material must exist at scale and who pays when the forecast is wrong, not how good the animation looks.

Megaprojects, as Oxford professor Bent Flyvbjerg defines them, are not just big buildings but transport, energy, water, IT, defence, health, science and events that cost over $1bn, run for years and are hard to reverse once started. Complexity, long horizons and irreversibility mean errors compound rather than cancel.

Flyvbjerg calls the pattern the Iron Law: over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over. Overruns of up to 50% are common and over 50% not uncommon. The tails are fatter than the averages suggest: Denver International Airport about 200% over, Boston's Big Dig about 220%, Sydney Opera House about 1,400%. For dams, delays average 44%, with each extra year adding about 4.6% to costs; for rail, average cost overrun is 44.7% while demand falls 51.4% short. The Channel Tunnel is the textbook case — about 80% over on construction in real terms, 140% over on financing, revenues about half forecast, a negative 14.5% return. Technologically it works; financially it destroyed value, with losses socialised when Eurotunnel went insolvent.

Performance has not improved in 70 years, across public and private sectors and geographies. The $1bn line is a conventional analytical bucket, not a legal threshold, and the 91.5%/8.5%/0.5% figures are snapshots from one large database, not natural constants. But the shape — systematic overrun with extreme outliers — is stable.

Why it happens is disputed. One driver is honest optimism bias: planners genuinely underestimate costs and overestimate benefits. The other is strategic misrepresentation: costs are low-balled and benefits inflated to win approval. Flyvbjerg documents both, but critics including Lavagnon Ika and Peter Love argue the distinction matters for the remedy and that lumping them overstates inevitability.

Two dynamics follow. Break-fix: projects break when reality hits, then are fixed at great cost because they cannot be abandoned. Survival-of-the-unfittest: the projects that look best on paper — the most underestimated — are the ones approved, so the worst bets are selected. Debt makes it worse, as delay drives cost growth and interest capitalises into a trap where income cannot cover costs. The Channel Tunnel and Sydney's Lane Cove Tunnel are cited examples.

Against this stands Albert Hirschman's Principle of the Hiding Hand, from 1967: decision-makers underestimate difficulties but also underestimate their creativity to solve them, so hidden problem-solving offsets hidden difficulty and initial ignorance is benevolent — without it, nothing ambitious would start. Flyvbjerg directly revisits the idea and calls it "unfounded and corrupting," arguing the offset does not hold empirically and encourages bad selection.

The evidence does not settle into fatalism. Flyvbjerg himself qualifies the Iron Law as a tendency, not destiny, pointing to the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao and Madrid's 2003-07 metro extension — 80 stations, 90km — built in about half the time and cost of comparable projects by standardising stations and running as many tunnel-boring machines and teams as needed. His prescription is "think slow, act fast": thorough front-end planning and frozen scope before the green light, then fast delivery, plus "find your LEGO" — scale-free modularity via repeatable modules, from solar cells to wind-turbine parts. Reference-class forecasting — comparing to a class of similar past projects rather than inside-view engineering estimates — is the antidote to optimism bias. None guarantees success; it improves odds. A credible minority caveat adds that the database may overstate inevitability through selection bias, overrepresenting troubled public projects that publish data and defining success narrowly as hitting all three targets at once.

That baseline gives you a ladder — built, underway, stalled, paper — a framing device, not an official taxonomy, that tracks material readiness and financing realism.

Built is China's Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze, built 1994-2012. At 22,500 megawatts from 32 turbines it is the world's largest hydro plant, 185 metres tall and 2.3km long, creating a 667km reservoir. Cost is put at about CNY207bn, roughly $29bn. It first generated power in 2003 and reached full capacity in 2012, with reported output around 88 terawatt-hours a year and a record above 111 in 2020, plus flood control and inland navigation to Chongqing. The full cost includes the largest hydropower resettlement in history — about 1.3 million people displaced — plus submerged heritage and ongoing landslide, seismic and ecological risks. Benefits — power revenues and avoided flood losses — have been tangible but unevenly distributed: downstream industry and the grid gain, resettled communities and ecology bear the loss. It shows Flyvbjerg's nuance: technological success is not the same as financial success, but inelastic demand for power and flood control can make a megadam viable if displacement is counted.

Underway is Saudi Arabia's The Line, the centrepiece of the 26,500 sq km NEOM zone in Tabuk Province developed by NEOM Company and its owner, the kingdom's $925bn sovereign fund, the Public Investment Fund. Announced as twin 500-metre mirrored walls, 200 metres apart, running 170km across desert for 9 million people, with zero cars and a 20-minute end-to-end trip, it has no precedent at full scale: continuous 500-metre structures over 170km, foundations and wind loads, cooling a 34 sq km canyon above 45C, desalination dependence and a mirrored facade in desert conditions. A $500bn figure was never detailed; later press reports cited $1.5tn for NEOM overall and $1.6tn to $4.5tn for The Line alone — press estimates, not audited budgets, with no audited spend published.

As of 2024-25 the status is earthworks on a short segment, no residents, with Sindalah island resort the most advanced part. Trojena's earthworks for the 2029 Asian Winter Games later saw the Games reassigned, with reports of about 35% workforce cuts. In April 2024 Bloomberg reported the 2030 target had been scaled back from 1.5 million people over 170km to fewer than 300,000 over a 2.4km initial segment, with the PIF budget not approved. Saudi officials pushed back. Economy Minister Faisal Al Ibrahim told CNBC at the World Economic Forum in Riyadh: "All projects are moving full steam ahead... There is no change in scale. It is a long-term project that's modular in design." Finance Minister Mohammed Al-Jadaan offered a more nuanced official view at the same meeting — "We will downscale some of the projects, we will accelerate other projects... We will change course and adjust as needed" — and by February 2026 the government described adjustment as portfolio prioritisation, with some projects scaled back, others expanded and some put on hold. Both framings can be true at once: a 2-3km segment is a very large mixed-use development — feasible but extraordinarily expensive — while 170km remains paper. That is the Iron Law meeting financing constraints as oil prices soften.

Paper is where material readiness fails. Japan's Shimizu Corporation proposed TRY 2004, a 2,004-metre pyramid in Tokyo Bay for 250,000 people, and its Ocean Spiral, a 500-metre floating sphere linked by a 15km spiral for 5,000 people, presented in 2014 as buildable in five years for about 3tn yen (about £16bn) once technology allowed in about 15 years. Architect Dante Bini said the pyramid was far above current technology; Shimizu framed both as future visions requiring carbon-nanotube-class strength and seismic engineering for a 2km structure in an earthquake-prone bay. Neither has funding or a timeline, and the 15-year horizon has not materialised.

The same block stops Obayashi Corporation's space elevator, announced in 2012 for 2050: a 96,000km ribbon well beyond geostationary orbit — about 36,000km up, where a satellite stays over one spot — to a counterweight, with climbers reaching orbit in about five hours at roughly 1/100th of launch cost. It needs a tether far stronger and lighter than steel — roughly 180 times stronger by one assessment — and even high-strength fibres would snap after a few hundred kilometres. Candidates like carbon nanotubes and graphene have been tested on the International Space Station, but defects and inability to spin continuous pure lengths mean no known material works at that length, and the 2050 date assumes a mass-production breakthrough with no demonstrated trajectory. Political risks — airspace, debris, liability if the ribbon snaps — are second-order to the material block.

Other paper giants follow the same split. Atlantropa, a 1928 plan to dam the Strait of Gibraltar and drop the Mediterranean 200 metres, attracted designs into the 1960s despite ecological and geopolitical absurdity. The 85km Bering Strait tunnel via the Diomede Islands recurs from 1904 to a 2014 Chinese high-speed rail fantasy to an October 2025 claim by Russian envoy Kirill Dmitriev of a joint US-Russia feasibility study at about $8bn in under eight years, floated on social media and reported as resting solely on him with no US confirmation — implausible against the Channel Tunnel's roughly $21bn today for 50km in benign geology, plus thousands of kilometres of missing rail across tundra and permafrost. Floating cities split similarly: UN-backed Oceanix Busan and the Maldives Floating City have modular prototypes and reported groundbreakings for thousands of residents, but scale-up is slowed by cost and regulation; mile-long ship concepts like Freedom Ship remain paper after 30 years. Small floating neighbourhoods are feasible; self-contained floating cities are not yet, with financing, insurance and flag-state law unsolved.

Cost-benefit analyses are systematically misleading, and overruns are paid by taxpayers on public projects or by equity holders wiped out in restructuring on private ones, while benefits arrive late and short. Winners tend to be contractors, consultants, nearby landowners and politicians who cut ribbons, and sometimes users who get a subsidised service. Losers are displaced people, taxpayers and investors.

Bets pay off when three conditions hold: demand is inelastic and large, as with Three Gorges power and flood control; delivery is modular and repeatable, as with Madrid's metro; and front-end planning is thorough with scope frozen — think slow, act fast — plus reference-class forecasting. NEOM concentrates risk on a single oil-exposed sovereign fund, now marketed via investor roadshows and a pivot to tourism, logistics, energy and events like the 2034 World Cup.

Place any giant on the ladder, ask what material must exist at scale and who is contractually on the hook when the forecast is wrong, and compare to a reference class that actually exists. If there is no reference class — a 2km pyramid, a 96,000km ribbon — you are not looking at a construction schedule. You are looking at a research programme.
