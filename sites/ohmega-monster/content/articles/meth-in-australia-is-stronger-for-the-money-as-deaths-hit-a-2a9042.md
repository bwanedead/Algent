Methamphetamine — sold in Australia mostly as crystal meth or ice — has become stronger for the money and easier to find, even as deaths, hospitalisations and wastewater traces all hit record highs in 2024. Most deaths were unintentional and concentrated among men in mid-life.

The picture comes from two long-running national trackers: the National Wastewater Drug Monitoring Program, run by the Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission, the federal agency that tracks organised crime, and overdose and drug-trend reporting led by the National Drug and Alcohol Research Centre at UNSW Sydney and the Penington Institute, a Melbourne-based public-health research group.

## More meth, despite record seizures

What changed is scale. The Commission estimates Australians consumed about 22.2 tonnes of four major illicit drugs in the 12 months to August 2024, up 34% as the market recovered after COVID and organised crime groups re-established and expanded. Methylamphetamine — the chemical name for methamphetamine — rose 21% to a record 12.8 tonnes.

Police seized more than 16 tonnes of methamphetamine in 2024, and for several major drugs the amount seized exceeded the amount estimated to have been consumed. The street value of the four drugs consumed in that wastewater year was put at $11.5 billion, with methylamphetamine accounting for $8.9 billion, about three-quarters.

Researchers say meth has fallen in price once purity is taken into account — in effect, stronger for the same money — and people who use drugs report it remains readily available. There is no published dollar-price and purity series by state or form, and no estimate of how much a price fall lifts use or deaths, so that is best read as a direction, not a precise drop. Australia is described as having one of the highest recorded rates of methamphetamine use in the world, which leaves it highly exposed when global supply expands.

There are two ways to read that cheaper, purer supply. One is that enforcement has failed to constrain availability. The other, which the Commission emphasises, is that a highly profitable global market is outpacing interdiction despite record seizures. In that view, seizures exceeding consumption for some drugs and large joint operations show real disruption that can create windows for demand and harm measures, rather than simply proving supply control does not work.

## Record harms, mostly unintentional

The human cost in 2024 was the highest on these measures. Penington put drug-induced deaths at 2,596, the highest on record and about 11% above 2,345 in 2023 — about seven a day. More than 2,000 were unintentional, the first time that threshold has been passed in its 24-year series, representing about 77,000 years of potential life lost, or about 31 years per person who died.

Stimulants were involved in 843 deaths, behind opioids and ahead of benzodiazepines, with more than one drug often involved. More than 750 deaths involving amphetamine-type stimulants, primarily methamphetamine, occurred at a rate of 2.9 per 100,000, up from around 2 per 100,000 in 2021-2023. Methamphetamine-related hospitalisations and wastewater-measured consumption were also at the highest observed levels. Most methamphetamine-related deaths were unintentional.

Harms are not evenly spread. Most methamphetamine-related deaths involved men aged 35 to 54. Over two decades harm has shifted to older age groups, with mortality rising since 2005 among people aged 25 to 54. At the population level, drug-induced deaths are among the leading causes of death for Australians in their 20s, 30s and 40s.

First Nations peoples — Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples — carry a disproportionately high burden of unintentional drug-induced deaths, and that disparity has widened sharply in recent years, which Penington flags as a national policy priority.

How to read the numbers needs care. The amphetamine-type stimulant counts are reported via syndication of the Research Centre's findings rather than directly from published tables. The headline total also differs by definition: the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, the federal health statistics agency, consolidates 1,948 drug-induced deaths in 2024, compared with Penington's 2,596, due to different case definitions and coding. The link between cheaper, purer meth and rising deaths and hospitalisations is presented without control for confounders — including the shift from powder to crystal, more frequent use and use of multiple drugs together — so it is a correlational pattern with a plausible economic mechanism, not a quantified causal effect. Wastewater monitoring since 2016 provides a consistent longitudinal signal now at a record, and the upward trajectory in deaths and consumption pre-dates COVID-19.

## Why there is no quick fix for meth

The Research Centre calls for a strong, coordinated response involving prevention, harm reduction, treatment access and ongoing monitoring. Penington notes that harm-minimisation efforts in several states and territories — including supervised injecting sites, drug checking and naloxone access — are succeeding where implemented but remain small-scale, accounting for about 2% of drug-policy funding on Penington's estimate, a figure contested as a framing device.

What can be promised for methamphetamine specifically is limited. Unlike opioids, where naloxone can reverse an overdose, there is no equivalent reversal agent for methamphetamine; care is supportive and engagement-based. Evidence for supervised injecting sites and drug checking is strongest for preventing opioid overdose and detecting contaminants, with more limited direct evidence that these interventions reduce methamphetamine deaths at population scale.

Treatment demand signals the need. Publicly funded alcohol and other drug treatment services provided about 244,000 treatment episodes to about 128,000 people aged 10 and over in 2024-25; alcohol was the principal drug of concern in 41% of episodes, methamphetamine second at 24%. Penington's recommended National Overdose Prevention Strategy spans education, naloxone, treatment access and drug checking. While naloxone is opioid-specific, the broader principles — early intervention, reducing harms from an unregulated supply and accessible treatment — are cited as relevant to stimulant harms where no reversal exists.

Australia's official framework is the National Drug Strategy 2017-2026, built on three pillars — demand reduction, supply reduction and harm reduction. Federal and state and territory laws prohibit possession and supply of illegal drugs including methamphetamine, but research with people who use drugs suggests illicit drugs remain readily available, indicating supply reduction alone has not constrained availability.

The supply-reduction and fiscal-sceptic case, steelmanned, runs like this: record consumption and cheaper, purer meth reflect a profitable global market where organised crime recovered after COVID and is outpacing interdiction, not proof that harm reduction has failed or that enforcement is pointless. Record seizures and joint operations show disruption that hinders criminal networks and creates windows for demand-reduction strategies. Reallocation toward harm reduction must weigh opportunity costs, recurrent costs, workforce and regulatory barriers to national scale-up, and the fact that for methamphetamine there is no naloxone equivalent and evidence for supervised consumption and drug checking is strongest for opioids and contaminants.

That tension — between a public-health case that deaths are largely unintentional and preventable and that coordinated harm reduction, treatment and monitoring reduce deaths where implemented, and a three-pillars case that supply disruption is real but insufficient against a glut and that scaling harm reduction nationally involves costs and limits, especially for stimulants — is the policy debate the record numbers force. Both sides point to the same market facts; they differ on what levers move deaths most, and how quickly.

Behind each number are people, families and communities. Health services encourage early help-seeking without judgement. People who use methamphetamine, families and friends seeking information or support can contact the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline on 1800 250 015 for free and confidential advice, or Lifeline on 13 11 14 for crisis support.
