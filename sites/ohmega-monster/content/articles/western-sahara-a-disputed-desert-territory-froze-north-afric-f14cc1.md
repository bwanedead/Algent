In late July 2026 about 50,000 to 60,000 people crossed in a single day from Morocco into Ceuta, a Spanish city of 85,000 on Africa's Mediterranean coast, overwhelming its streets and shelters before most turned back within days. The surge dramatized a much older freeze: a 50-year rivalry over Western Sahara — a disputed desert territory on Africa's Atlantic coast, a former Spanish colony claimed by Morocco and sought as an independent state by the Sahrawi Polisario movement backed by Algeria — that has kept the Algeria-Morocco land border shut since 1994, left a regional union without a summit in 30 years, and now forces Europe to weigh two competing dependencies at once. Whether Morocco deliberately engineered the Ceuta rush as diplomatic leverage or simply allowed it by relaxing controls remains contested, and whether the latest UN shift toward autonomy will bring negotiations or a boycott is still open.

The map helps. Western Sahara sits on the northwest edge of Africa, south of Morocco proper. Since 1991 a UN peacekeeping mission called MINURSO — the United Nations Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara, created to hold a ceasefire and organize a vote on the territory's future — has patrolled a sand berm that divides roughly 80% under Moroccan control from the rest, with Sahrawi refugees living in camps near Tindouf in Algeria. Ceuta, 1,000 kilometers north across the Strait of Gibraltar, is EU territory in Africa, fenced and walled off from Morocco by land and sea. The Arab Maghreb Union, founded in 1989 to bind Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, Libya and Mauritania, has not held a summit in three decades.

## The referendum that never happened

Spain withdrew from Spanish Sahara in 1975. Morocco sent 350,000 civilians south in the Green March to claim the territory, and Morocco and Mauritania tried to partition it. The Polisario Front, the Sahrawi independence movement that runs a government-in-exile called the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic and is considered by the UN as the representative of the Sahrawi people, fought a 16-year war with Morocco with Algerian backing. The Moroccan government had taken its claim to the International Court of Justice as a historic "Greater Morocco"; the Court disagreed and called for self-determination for those who lived there. Days later Spain signed the Madrid Accords and transferred administration to Morocco and Mauritania without providing for that vote.

That war ended not with a settlement but with a pause. In 1991 the UN brokered a ceasefire and created MINURSO to keep it and to organize a referendum on self-determination. The vote never happened. Disputes over who could vote and, from 2003 onward, Morocco's refusal to take part in any referendum that included an independence option froze the process. The UN still lists Western Sahara as Africa's last non-self-governing territory. The ceasefire itself collapsed on 13 November 2020, when Moroccan troops entered the UN-monitored buffer strip at Guerguerat to clear a pro-Polisario blockade, after which Polisario declared the ceasefire over and resumed attacks.

## Why neither side can give way

The territory matters beyond sand and phosphate because each capital has tied it to a domestic logic that makes compromise structurally hard.

For Morocco, Western Sahara is territorial integrity, central to the monarchy's legitimacy and to a national narrative of completing decolonization by recovering historic lands. Its offer since 2007 has been autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty: devolved legislative, executive and judicial bodies elected by residents, with Rabat keeping defense, foreign affairs, currency, nationality and the supreme judiciary. A 40-page expanded statute presented in Madrid in February 2026 detailed a unicameral regional parliament, a regional high court and fiscal arrangements, to be entrenched constitutionally.

For Algeria, which defines itself as an anti-colonial state, backing Sahrawi self-determination is ideological and strategic. Algeria supports the Polisario's demand for a referendum that includes independence, a position rooted in the International Court of Justice's 1975 advisory opinion that found no ties of sovereignty between Morocco and Western Sahara sufficient to override self-determination, and reinforced by African Union recognition of the Sahrawi republic.

Analysts at the Madrid-based Research Unit on International Security and Cooperation describe this as a struggle for regional hegemony, not a negotiable border. That framing does not make the positions immutable national characters, but it explains why Western Sahara has become a symbol of who leads the Maghreb.

## A border shut, a union dead

The rivalry has concrete costs. The land border has been closed since 1994, after a hotel attack in Marrakech led to visa expulsions. Diplomatic ties, restored in 1988, were cut again on 24 August 2021, when Algeria severed relations citing hostile acts including alleged Moroccan support for the Kabylia separatist movement MAK, and closed its airspace to Moroccan aircraft.

Energy followed. Algeria closed the Maghreb-Europe Gas Pipeline on 1 November 2021 when the contract expired; the pipe has sat empty since, used only in reverse to send gas from Spain to Morocco, while the direct Medgaz pipeline now carries most Algerian gas to Spain.

The Arab Maghreb Union, meant to integrate the five states, is described by analysts as collapsed rather than merely stalled — an analytic judgment, not a formal dissolution — with no summit in 30 years and trade between Algeria and Morocco among the lowest between neighbours anywhere. That paralysis weakens collective bargaining on migration, energy and Sahel security.

## Two opposite turns after 2020

Since 2020 the dispute has internationalized in opposite directions, pulling European institutions two ways at once.

On the geopolitical track, Morocco normalized relations with Israel on 10 December 2020 as part of the Abraham Accords, in exchange for a U.S. proclamation recognizing Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara. Israel recognized that claim on 17 July 2023, and the two signed a defense memorandum in November 2021 — the first such security and intelligence cooperation agreement between Israel and any Arab country, signed by Israeli Defense Minister Benny Gantz in Rabat.

Algeria views that prospect of long-term Israeli military and intelligence cooperation as a threat to the regional balance, a classic security dilemma described by the International Crisis Group: each side sees its own modernization as defensive and the other's as threatening, fueling mistrust and an arms buildup even though neither seeks open war.

What that buildup looks like is now better documented. Data from the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute cited by the Jerusalem Post shows Israel was Morocco's third-largest source of defense imports between 2019 and 2023, accounting for 11% of procurement even though relations were only established in 2020. A CIDOB report by the Barcelona Centre for International Affairs, summarized by Morocco World News, puts the same share at about 11% for 2020-2024 and lists contracts including Barak MX air-defense systems worth $500 million, a $1 billion agreement for two Ofek 13 reconnaissance satellites, and a BlueBird Aero Systems loitering-munitions factory inaugurated in Benslimane in November 2025. The Jerusalem Post separately reported Morocco's successful trial of an Israeli Extra guided missile — a 306mm rocket with 150km range — fired from Elbit Systems PULS launchers procured in a $150 million deal, and noted Morocco already operates BlueBird SpyX and Elbit Skystriker drones, with BlueBird's WonderB and ThunderB systems and a local assembly line, plus a July 2024 purchase of Ofek 13 satellites from Israel Aerospace Industries instead of previous French suppliers Airbus and Thales, and reported purchases of Atmos howitzers and Barak 8 and Spider air-defense systems. Algeria is still assessed as retaining overall superiority — CIDOB notes Algeria spent $21.8 billion on defense in 2024, 21% of its public budget, versus Morocco's $5.5 billion — but the direction of Morocco's sourcing has shifted.

On the legal track, Europe's top court moved the other way. On 4 October 2024 the Grand Chamber of the Court of Justice of the European Union, in Joined Cases C-779/21 P and C-799/21 P, upheld the annulment of EU-Morocco trade and fisheries agreements as applied to Western Sahara, finding the territory separate and distinct from Morocco and requiring the consent of its people — obtained via the Polisario as UN-recognized representative — for deals to cover it. The court ordered effects maintained for 12 months to 4 October 2025.

Then the Security Council tilted back toward geopolitics. On 31 October 2025 it adopted Resolution 2797, 11 votes in favour with three abstentions — China, Russia and Pakistan — while Algeria, then a non-permanent member, did not participate. The text extended MINURSO to 31 October 2026 and called on parties to engage on the basis of Morocco's autonomy proposal, stating that genuine autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty could be the most feasible solution. The CIDOB analysis treats the resolution as a structural inflection point, noting Morocco's foreign minister Nasser Bourita visited Moscow on 16 October 2025 to seek assurances Russia would not veto, and that King Mohammed VI called the vote a historic moment with a "before and after October 31."

## What self-determination means here

That phrase is where the law splits. Three readings now coexist.

Morocco's foreign minister, Nasser Bourita, put the autonomy view plainly in December 2025: "no one has ever said that self-determination is synonymous with a referendum," arguing it can be expressed through negotiated agreement and signature within autonomy. King Mohammed VI called Resolution 2797 a historic new chapter in consolidating the Moroccanness of the Sahara and urged refugees in Tindouf to endorse autonomy.

The Polisario and its supporters read self-determination as external — a referendum with independence as an option. After the vote, Polisario said it will not take part in "any peace process or negotiations based on proposals that aim to legitimize the Moroccan military occupation" and deprive Sahrawis of self-determination, while its UN representative Sidi Omar said the resolution "does not imply any recognition of Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara." Algeria's ambassador Amar Bendjama told the Council "The final decision on the future cannot, must not, belong to anyone other than the people under colonial domination," adding that the text "ignores the proposals of the Polisario Front."

Scholars at EJIL:Talk, Western Sahara Resource Watch and Human Rights Watch stress a third, procedural caveat: autonomy would require the free consent of the Sahrawi people — not a settler majority — and self-determination as a peremptory norm cannot be overridden by a Council resolution. By referencing only Morocco's proposal without equal reference to Polisario's 2007 and 2025 referendum proposals, they argue, the Council risks the perception of implicit recognition. Former UN envoy Christopher Ross called the resolution a step backward favouring one party. None of this makes autonomy settled law; the 2007 initiative and 2026 statute remain proposals, not implementation.

## Spain caught in the middle

Spain, the former colonial power, shows how the two tracks collide in practice. In 2022 Madrid abandoned decades of neutrality to back Morocco's autonomy plan, prompting Algeria to suspend its 2002 friendship treaty in June 2022, recall its ambassador and freeze banking operations linked to trade.

Madrid then tried a dual track: deepening cooperation with Rabat on migration and counterterrorism while repairing with Algiers. On 20 July 2026 Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez — Spain's Socialist premier — made his first visit to Algeria in four years, meeting President Abdelmadjid Tebboune and Prime Minister Sifi Ghrieb at the El Mouradia Palace in Algiers. Sánchez said the visit held "particular importance" and that Algeria was "not only a neighbouring country but also a strategic partner," noting he was accompanied by senior Spanish energy executives and that Algeria was Spain's largest supplier of natural gas this year. Tebboune called talks "fruitful" and "frank" and said they gave relations a "new impetus," discussing energy, Spanish investment, migration and Western Sahara. The two sides agreed to political consultations and to hold an 8th High-Level Meeting in October 2026. La Moncloa, the Spanish prime minister's office, highlighted that Algeria was Spain's main natural gas supplier so far in 2026. The visit did not resolve the rupture; it opened a new phase after Algeria had reactivated the friendship treaty in March 2026 and commercial ties had begun to restore the previous year.

## 60,000 crossings in a city of 85,000

Against that repair effort came Ceuta. Around 30-31 July 2026, 50,000 to 60,000 people crossed from Morocco into the exclave in about 24 hours — about 70% of the city's population — by swimming around breakwaters and via the Tarajal crossing. Spain's Interior Ministry put the figure at about 50,000 since the previous Thursday; Ceuta's president Juan Jesús Vivas said about 60,000. Many swam several miles from the Moroccan towns of Fnideq and Belyounech.

At least 57 people died on 31 July, some drowned and some killed in a stampede at the Tarajal breakwater, according to Spanish officials and the Associated Press. By 3 August the official balance reported by Reuters was 72 deaths on the Spanish side and 11 on the Moroccan side. Figures vary without forensic verification, and no single precise range is established.

Most returned quickly. The Interior Ministry said 48,300 had gone back voluntarily by the evening of 1 August; Reuters, citing Spanish estimates on 3 August, put returns at about 69,500 over four days. Between 3,000 and 5,000 remained, overwhelming Ceuta's two reception centres — one for adults and one for minors — which local officials said had collapsed. The city was processing 862 unaccompanied minors, who have special protections from deportation. The pattern echoed the 2021 Ceuta surge.

Why it happened is contested, and the debate has three positions. El Pais entertained that Morocco deliberately allowed the crossings to coincide with Sánchez's Algeria trip as migration leverage, calling any use of immigration as a bargaining chip unacceptable. Moroccan Interior Ministry spokesman Rachid El Khalfi rejected accusations of deliberate facilitation, blaming malicious exploitation of digital space, misleading information and misinterpretation of a recent Spanish Supreme Court ruling that migrants arriving by sea could not be summarily returned.

A middle view has converged among DW-interviewed experts and in Spanish press reports citing intelligence: Morocco did not plan the surge but allowed it by gradually relaxing border controls in July and removing them during Throne Day holiday crowds at Tarajal — border management as diplomatic signal. Reuters on 3 August reported that El Pais and Cadena SER, citing officials familiar with the matter, said Spanish intelligence had concluded Morocco did not plan the mass crossing but allowed it to happen, with controls relaxed during July and removed at Tarajal during Throne Day. That intelligence assessment remains reported via those outlets and is unconfirmed by a primary document, and experts told DW it is unlikely Morocco intended to instigate a major diplomatic incident. The Moroccan Interior Ministry did not immediately respond to Reuters' request for comment, while saying Morocco is committed to combating irregular migration.

## Energy against migration

The Ceuta episode crystallizes a wider European dilemma described by the European Council on Foreign Relations. Morocco offers the EU gatekeeping on irregular migration, counterterrorism and economic cooperation; Algeria offers reliable gas and Sahel security influence. Brussels favouring one risks alienating the other, as Spain learned between 2022 and 2026, with France moving closer to Rabat while Germany courts Algiers. The CJEU's legal principle of separate and distinct territory and the Security Council's geopolitical pragmatism pull EU institutions in opposite directions, which is why Spain's oscillation looks less like inconsistency than rational balancing under contradictory pressures.

The security logic reinforces the stalemate. With the 2020 ceasefire broken, drone strikes reported in Western Sahara and Sahel instability after the 2023 Niger coup, the International Crisis Group warns that even minor incidents or miscalculations could escalate, even without intent for open war.

## Rights without a monitor

Beneath the diplomacy is a monitoring gap that leaves legitimacy claims hard to test.

In Moroccan-controlled Western Sahara, Human Rights Watch and Sahrawi groups document systematic obstruction of self-determination advocacy: laws penalizing affronts to territorial integrity, prevention of gatherings, surveillance, phone tapping, arbitrary detention, beatings and imprisonment after unfair trials, including 19 men jailed after the Gdeim Izik protest camp. In 2024-2025, groups including CODESA, the Sahrawi Association of Victims of Grave Human Rights Violations, and Equipe Media reported members subjected to checkpoint harassment, car vandalism and detention — including journalists Ahmed Ettanji and Mohamed Mayara questioned at Cape Bojador on 9 October 2024 and activist Hassan Zerouali detained in early 2025 — while ACAPS and CODESA documented 14 arrests in 2024 including 10-year sentences for Sahrawi students Hussein Bourkab and Ayman Yathribi. The UN Secretary-General's July 2025 report cited intimidation and discrimination against Sahrawis advocating self-determination, and the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights has been denied access since 2015. Eight UN Special Rapporteurs in June 2025 denounced repression and home demolitions.

In the Tindouf camps, Human Rights Watch's 2013-2014 field research found no pattern of grave abuse but identified concerns: military courts trying civilians, prolonged pre-trial detention without court orders, isolated torture allegations, persistence of slavery-like domestic servitude in a small minority despite a 2011 legal ban, and one enforced disappearance — El Khalil Ahmed in Algiers in 2009. Algeria remains ultimately responsible under international law for rights on its territory despite ceding de facto administration to Polisario.

Humanitarian conditions in the camps remain acute: a June 2025 UN press release cited global acute malnutrition at 13.6%, 65% of children and 69% of women of reproductive age anemic, one-third of children stunted, with only 34% of funding for the Sahrawi Refugee Response Plan received. UNHCR estimates about 173,000 refugees in five camps.

MINURSO itself is the only modern UN peacekeeping mission without a human rights mandate — opposed by Morocco, favoured by Polisario and NGOs — which is why both sets of allegations lack independent UN monitoring.

What remains open is whether the UN's 2025 language changes anything on the ground. Polisario and Algeria have rejected the autonomy basis; Morocco presents it as realism endorsed by Washington and now the Council; European courts insist on Sahrawi consent; and Spain must keep both gas flowing and borders managed. Until a process squares those definitions of self-determination — autonomy as internal self-rule versus referendum with independence — the desert dispute will keep freezing the region and spilling, periodically, across the Strait.
