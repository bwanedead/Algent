Lebanon has started pilot-zone operations in three southern villages — Froun, Srifa and Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh — under a U.S.-brokered trilateral framework.

The State Department said on July 20 that the launch began under the auspices of the Military Coordination Group for Lebanon and was a direct outcome of talks between Israel and Lebanon in Rome. Reuters said the June 26 framework deal explicitly links Hezbollah disarmament, the deployment of Lebanese troops in the south and the progressive withdrawal of Israeli forces.

On the ground, the rollout is not uniform. Reuters reported that Lebanese army units were continuing missions in Froun and Srifa, while deployment in Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh was tied to Israeli withdrawal from the area. A separate Reuters report on July 21 said Lebanese troops began deploying in Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh after Israeli forces withdrew, describing the move as the first test of the plan. BBC reporting likewise said the army entered Zawtar al-Gharbiyeh after withdrawal, while the two other villages were already part of the pilot-zone announcement.

The significance is sovereignty, not just movement. Official and wire reporting frame the pilot zones as a step toward reasserting Lebanese state authority in areas where Hezbollah has long been the dominant armed actor. Reuters said the Lebanese army would be responsible for clearing the zones of Hezbollah weapons and infrastructure, while an Israeli military official said the pilot was a test of the Lebanese Armed Forces’ sovereignty in the three villages, as the sole official authority authorized to bear arms there.

Hezbollah’s public counter-frame is narrower than a blanket rejection of Lebanese army deployment. In a statement on Al-Manar, Hezbollah Secretary General Sheikh Naim Qassem said there should be “no safe zones for ‘Israel’, and no yellow, red, or green zones.” He also said disarming the Resistance “can never be discussed,” while adding that the Lebanese Army should deploy “exclusively south of the Litani River” within the November 27 agreement and that Hezbollah had already facilitated the army’s deployment in the south.

What remains unclear is what will decide whether this becomes a real transfer of control or only a new label for an old balance. The accessible sources do not spell out the full Lebanese-side legal authority, the detailed rules of engagement, or whether the three villages are a narrow pilot or the start of a broader security shift. For now, the evidence shows the framework, the first deployments and the dispute over armed authority — but not yet a durable end state.
