Ceuta is a Spanish autonomous city on Morocco’s northern coast and part of the European Union’s external border. Spain controls what happens after people arrive—border security, reception and legal procedures—but preventing crossings and arranging returns depend heavily on cooperation with Morocco.

That system came under exceptional pressure in late July. More than 1,500 people had reached Ceuta during the preceding week, according to the city government. On July 30 and 31, the movement accelerated: people swam around the Tarajal breakwater, crossed or bypassed land-border infrastructure and reached the city from Morocco. The route involved several kilometres in the water, rocks and improvised flotation devices. Rescue operations continued as reception facilities filled; people were reported sleeping in streets and parks. The latest RTVE update consulted reported 34 deaths, but earlier reports gave lower figures and no reconciled official casualty account was available.

The scale estimates were also unstable. Reports and statements from Spanish authorities put the main episode at roughly 40,000 to 60,000 arrivals. Those numbers may include attempted crossings, repeated entries or people who later returned to Morocco; they should not be read as a settled count of distinct people remaining in Ceuta. For comparison, Spain’s Interior Ministry recorded 2,582 irregular land arrivals into Ceuta during the first half of 2026 and no maritime arrivals. Ceuta has about 84,000 residents. Even the lower July estimate would therefore represent a shock far beyond the city’s ordinary border and reception arrangements, without establishing a permanent increase in its population.

Spain responded by reinforcing the civilian system rather than handing the border to the army. The Armed Forces were placed under the military operations command to support the Guardia Civil, Spain’s force responsible for border and maritime security. The wider reinforcement included additional Guardia Civil personnel, diving and maritime units, a patrol vessel, a helicopter and police reinforcements that were expected to reach about 200 UIP and UPR officers. The exact military personnel total, duration, tasking and rules of engagement for the July operation have not been published in the evidence available here. Nothing established that soldiers independently carried out immigration returns or replaced the Guardia Civil’s command.

The immediate cause of the surge remains disputed. Spain’s Interior Ministry said human-smuggling networks had exploited or misrepresented a Supreme Court ruling issued on July 8. The ruling did not create a general right to remain in Spain. It addressed a narrower question: people intercepted at sea while trying to swim into Ceuta or Melilla cannot be dealt with under the special border-rejection regime designed for people caught while crossing physical border barriers. They must instead go through the ordinary return procedure, which requires an individualized assessment and legal safeguards. The distinction does not apply identically to land crossings.

Some migrants interviewed by the media appeared to believe that reaching Ceuta by sea would protect them from return; others said they had not heard of the ruling. That supports the existence of confusion and rapidly circulating information, but it does not show that a smuggling network organized the July movement or spread the relevant claim. Spain’s documented police operation against a Ceuta-linked smuggling network was announced in June 2025. It involved eight arrests, searches, seized boats and allegations of at least 200 clandestine entries, but the case material does not connect that network to July 2026, the Supreme Court ruling or a misinformation campaign.

A second explanation focuses on Morocco. Reports described Moroccan forces as initially absent, passive or unable to stop large groups, followed by stronger controls. The episode also came about a week after Pedro Sánchez, Spain’s prime minister, visited Algeria, Morocco’s regional rival. Analysts pointed to May 2021, when roughly 9,000 to 12,000 people entered Ceuta during a severe Spain–Morocco dispute after Spain treated Brahim Ghali, the leader of the Polisario Front. That precedent makes Moroccan enforcement and bilateral pressure relevant questions. It does not establish that Rabat deliberately opened the border, ordered the movement or used migration as coercion in July 2026. Other reporting described Moroccan efforts to stop people travelling north and suggested that the security response may itself have been overwhelmed.

The return agreement between Spain and Morocco does not make every return automatic. The 1992 readmission instrument requires a formal request, proof that a person came from Morocco and supporting identity and entry information, normally within 10 days. It also contains exclusions, including for people recognized as refugees or authorized to remain in Spain. Spain and Morocco said they would cooperate on returns after the crossings, but the available records do not establish how many people were returned, under which instrument or how maritime arrivals were processed after the Supreme Court ruling.

Unaccompanied Moroccan minors face an additional framework. The 2007 agreement requires protection under Spanish and international law, prior agreement on each proposed return and the number of minors involved, and arrangements for family reunification or reception by a Moroccan tutelary institution. Ceuta’s official ordinary capacity for its protection and guardianship system for unaccompanied foreign minors was 29 places in 2026. That figure is not the capacity of the city’s main migrant reception centre, known as the CETI, and does not show how many minors arrived or whether the system was full during the surge. No operational record identified in the available material shows how the minors’ agreement was applied to a July case.

The episode therefore left Spain with three separate problems rather than one. It had to restore control and rescue people on a route where deaths were being reported; it had to process arrivals under procedures that differ according to how and where they were intercepted; and it had to rely on Morocco while lacking a settled public account of why Moroccan controls failed or weakened.

The unresolved facts are concrete: the number of distinct arrivals, attempted and repeat crossings, people who remained after returns, deaths and causes of death, the number of minors and asylum applicants, and the legal procedure used for each category. The evidence supports an overwhelmed border-control and reception system. It does not yet settle whether the surge was driven mainly by trafficking-linked information, a breakdown or relaxation of Moroccan control, bilateral signalling, or a combination of those factors.
