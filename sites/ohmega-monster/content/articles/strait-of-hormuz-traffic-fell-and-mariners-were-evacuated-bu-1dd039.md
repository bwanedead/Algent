The Strait of Hormuz is a maritime chokepoint: the narrow sea lane between Iran and Oman that carries commercial shipping and energy flows. When it is under pressure, the question is not just who has the upper hand. It is whether ships can still move, and at what risk.

The U.S. side said its strikes were aimed at that problem. CENTCOM said on July 8 that it struck Iranian military targets to further degrade Iran’s ability to attack commercial shipping and innocent civilian mariners in the Strait of Hormuz. On July 12, it said it hit dozens of targets at multiple locations to degrade Iran’s ability to keep attacking shipping through the strait, and said U.S. forces were prepared to ensure freedom of navigation for commercial shipping. On June 27, CENTCOM said the strikes were a direct response to continued Iranian aggression against commercial shipping and targeted surveillance, communications, air defenses, drone storage, and minelayer capabilities.

Iran pushed the opposite message. Reuters reported that Iranian sources described Hormuz as an inviolable red line, said reopening depended on U.S. compliance with Iranian regulations and a memorandum of understanding, and warned that Iran could strike infrastructure across the Gulf if its own infrastructure was hit. Reuters also reported that President Donald Trump said the strait was open to commercial traffic.

What the record verifies most clearly is disruption, not a clean blockade. Reuters reported that tanker traffic through Hormuz fell to the lowest level in two months, with shipping sources saying vessels were increasingly switching off their AIS transponders, the ships’ broadcast tracking data used by other vessels and traffic managers. JMIC said commercial traffic continued at reduced levels and that traffic patterns reflected operator caution after recent attacks. Its advisories also said transit remained possible, with specific routing and mine-risk precautions, and described the threat level as moderate.

The strongest institutional corroboration is the IMO’s response. It said it would begin implementing an evacuation plan for more than 11,000 stranded seafarers in cooperation with Iran, Oman, other coastal states, the United States and the maritime industry. The IMO’s focus on evacuating stranded seafarers suggests disruption and heightened risk, but it does not confirm that the strait was continuously shut. The IMO framed the move as restoring maritime security and ending attacks on civilian shipping; the text read here did not mention a Hormuz blockade.

The unresolved parts matter too. The verified releases do not establish the full scale of damage to civilian or dual-use infrastructure. They do not, in the text read here, publish a formal legal memorandum or Article 51, a UN Charter self-defense provision often cited to justify uses of force, style justification. And the UN human-rights office said reported attacks on commercial ships and civilian infrastructure must stop immediately, warned that a closure of Hormuz would have severe humanitarian consequences, and urged a return to the ceasefire consistent with international law.

So the best-supported reading is this: Hormuz was pressured, constrained and treated as dangerous enough to trigger route management and evacuation planning. What the sources verified here do not prove is a durable, physically sustained closure.
