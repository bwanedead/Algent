The Supreme Court of India has taken up a Shiv Sena (UBT) challenge to the Lok Sabha Speaker’s recognition of six rebel MPs’ merger with the Eknath Shinde-led Shiv Sena, but only at the notice stage. It issued notice, asked the Speaker’s office and other respondents to reply, and refused immediate interim relief.

That means the court has engaged with the dispute, but it has not yet ruled on the merits or set any new precedent. The matter is listed to return after two weeks.

The case sits inside India’s anti-defection framework, the constitutional rules that decide whether an MP can keep their seat when they break party lines; here, the Speaker’s first job is to recognize or reject the change in faction status before the legal consequences follow. The Lok Sabha Speaker is the presiding officer of the lower house, and in this dispute the Speaker’s office is the first institutional decision-point under challenge: the petitioners say the Speaker had no jurisdiction to recognize the merger, while the respondent side says existing Constitution Bench law already answers those objections.

Those are still hearing-stage arguments, not settled doctrine. The reports also show a sharper institutional question underneath them: how far the Court can scrutinize the Speaker’s handling before any final merits ruling, rather than simply letting the parliamentary process run its course.

The political stakes are real, even if the precise arithmetic remains only partly verified here. One report said the merger lifted the Shinde-led Shiv Sena’s strength in the Lok Sabha from 7 to 13, which would change how much floor strength and bargaining leverage that bloc has in the house. But the exact legal effect on voting power or seat status is not established in this run, so that should be treated cautiously.

What is clear is narrower and more important: the court has opened the door to review, but it has not yet walked through it.
