U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement appears to have entered a sharper interior-enforcement phase in mid-2026.

In June, ICE booked people into detention at record levels for the Trump administration, and in the first 11 days of July it was arresting people even faster, averaging 1,593 arrests a day. ICE’s June average was about 1,300 arrests a day, and the agency said it made more than 43,000 arrests that month.

The detention pool is changing in composition as well as size. Published reporting on ICE’s latest data put detainees without criminal convictions at 37% of the total, while another account said less than one-third had a criminal conviction and about 40% had never been accused of anything beyond non-criminal immigration violations. ICE’s own statistics page shows why the shorthand can mislead: its “no convictions or pending charges” bucket also includes repeat immigration violators, people with final orders of removal (meaning an immigration judge has ordered removal and the case is no longer pending), and foreign fugitives wanted for crimes abroad.

That is also where the administration’s defense comes in. ICE says it detains people to secure their presence for immigration proceedings and removal, and also detains those subject to mandatory detention or judged to be flight or public-safety risks. DHS, its parent department, says ICE’s duties focus on smart immigration enforcement, humane detention, preventing terrorism, and combating the illegal movement of people and goods.

The public record is hard to read cleanly. ICE says its enforcement statistics are published one quarter in arrears and may later be corrected or superseded, and the July 20 release on detention numbers came after a roughly three-month delay. Reporting also says broader DHS enforcement datasets had not been updated since the end of 2024.

The key downstream comparison is still missing. One report says completed removals — the people ICE actually sends out of the country after the case reaches the removal stage — stayed roughly flat even as arrests and detention rose, but the profile does not verify a current primary removals figure from ICE’s downloadable data. ICE’s removal office is a multi-step pipeline that runs through field offices, ICE Air Operations, Removal Management, International Operations, and other partners, so a slower removals line could reflect logistics as much as output. But that remains an inference until the monthly removals numbers are checked directly.

Courts still appear to be imposing some constraint. Reporting says bond hearings — court proceedings that can let some detained immigrants be released while their cases continue — are freeing about 2,000 detainees a month, and judges have resisted parts of the administration’s mandatory-detention posture. The exact legal and geographic scope of that check is not fully verified here, so it should be read as a real but only partly measured brake.

The surge also has a safety and accountability edge. ICE vehicle stops remained part of the campaign after deadly July shootings, and reporting said Tom Homan indicated officers would wear at least one body camera during vehicle stops. That does not amount to a fully verified agency-wide directive, but it does show the roadside stop has become a flashpoint.

The administration’s messaging that ICE is focused on criminals is broader than the “criminals only” slogan suggests. In July, ICE arrested Wu Shaoping, a Chinese human-rights lawyer who had an asylum claim pending after fleeing China in 2019, while he was delivering parcels in Pennsylvania. It is one case, not a statistical profile, but it shows the campaign can reach asylum seekers and rights defenders as well as people with criminal records.

The simplest accurate picture is this: ICE is arresting and detaining more people, the detained population includes a substantial share without criminal convictions, and the public data trail is delayed enough that the removals question is still unresolved. The surge is real; the clean accounting of what it produces is not.

**How this piece is framed:** The story is first about a verified mid-2026 acceleration in ICE interior enforcement — record bookings/arrests and a rising detained population — and only secondarily about what delayed, revision-prone public data can and cannot tell us about downstream outcomes like removals.
