In the southern Gulf of California, researchers documented two orca encounters with sharp-tail sunfish that are unusual for what the animals did, not just what they ate.

In each case, one orca held a sharp-tail sunfish in place — the paper says the hold was on the fish’s clavus, the tail-base structure — while another came in at speed and struck it hard enough to fragment the tissue. The paper calls this a “ram-to-fragment” behavior and says it was previously undescribed for orcas. The study also says the animals were not simply killing the fish: in the first event, the sunfish was already dead and its viscera had been removed before the impact.

The first encounter was filmed on July 29, 2024 near an offshore seamount off San José del Cabo, Baja California Sur, Mexico. The second was observed on September 7, 2025 in the Isla Cerralvo channel. In the first event, an adult female held the sunfish by the clavus while an adult male rammed it; a juvenile then fed on the smaller suspended fragments. In the second, one adult stabilized the fish while another rammed it, and a calf later fed on tissue from the carcass.

The concrete dispute here is whether this repeated sequence is just flexible prey-processing learned within a family or social group, or something stronger: a social-learning or cultural tactic passed among orcas. The footage shows the sequence itself; it does not show identifiable individuals across both events, so it cannot settle that larger claim.

That is the strongest case for reading the behavior as prey processing: the impact broke the prey into smaller pieces, and in the first event a juvenile took advantage of those fragments. The authors also argue the sequence may help younger orcas feed more easily, and they leave open other non-exclusive explanations, including social interaction and play.

Two observed events are enough to document a real pattern, but not enough to prove a stable tradition. The study cannot tie the encounters to identified individuals or a named pod, and it says high-resolution top-side images were not available across both events, so it could not determine whether the same orcas were involved.

Orcas are known to use coordinated prey-handling tactics in other settings, and the Gulf of California already has a history of orca–molid interactions. So the new footage fits within a broader picture of flexible foraging. But the evidence here still only supports a narrower claim: the Baja orcas showed a repeated hold-and-ram sequence that may be a feeding tactic, while social learning or culture remains a plausible hypothesis rather than a demonstrated fact.
