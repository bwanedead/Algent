On Sept. 18 President Donald Trump said on Truth Social the United States had an agreement with Denmark and Greenland giving Washington "permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland" at "NO COST" and for "Infinite Life," with "complete ability to do what is necessary" forever, a veto over any adversary base or sensitive investment without US written approval, and an immediate start on a large military presence.

The other two governments described something narrower the same day. Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen — head of government of the European kingdom that includes Greenland — said she was glad to be about to make "a great agreement for Greenland, the Kingdom of Denmark, and the United States" that strengthens common security in the Arctic and North Atlantic and "recognizes the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom and the Greenlandic people's right to self-determination." Greenland's premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen said the agreement "recognizes Greenland's interests and our place in the international cooperation." Neither repeated "permanent control," "Infinite Life," "NO COST" or the veto. No text, annex or map of defense areas had been published as of Sept. 19-20.

The most expansive details have come not in joint language but in briefings by unnamed US officials to Fox News and CBS: permanent access, basing and overflight rights; the ability to add installations after consultation without Danish or Greenlandic approval; a bar on China, Russia and other non-NATO states basing troops; and US written approval for sensitive investments in telecom, undersea cables, ports and critical minerals, with no expiration and survival even if Greenland becomes independent. Until a text appears, the deal is best read as a political framework, not a ratified treaty.

That distinction matters because the baseline already favored Washington. The 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement, concluded at NATO's request, let the US build and operate installations in defense areas in Greenland while preserving Denmark's sovereignty. Thule Air Base — now Pituffik Space Base on the northwest coast — was built in 1951-53. At its Cold War peak the US kept about 10,000 troops at up to 17 sites.

A 2004 amendment signed at Igaliku updated the framework for Greenland's growing autonomy, named Pituffik as the only defense area at that time while keeping a procedure to add more, and formalized consultation with Greenland's Home Rule Government.

Today the footprint is one base: Pituffik, about 150 personnel, operating an Upgraded Early Warning Radar that feeds missile warning and space surveillance to NORAD, the US-Canadian North American Aerospace Defense Command, plus Greenland's position astride the Greenland-Iceland-UK Gap where NATO tracks Russian submarines entering the Atlantic. The Danish Institute for International Studies describes this as "extensive access and freedom of action" in a "mature theater governed by formal cooperation" — which is why analysts split on whether the new language is a legal revolution or a restatement of what notification already allowed. The US drew down by choice, not because the treaty barred expansion, and whether unilateral expansion without approval is new is contested.

On paper, the claimed upgrades beyond 1951 as briefed — not jointly endorsed — are three: permanence and exclusivity with no end date and survival after independence plus a US veto on adversary bases and investments; unilateral expansion after consultation without needing approval; and investment gatekeeping over telecom, cables, ports and minerals to prevent a commercial foothold like the Panama Canal precedent the administration cites.

*How Greenland's mineral endowment and US footprint compare to the claims around them. The U.S. Geological Survey's Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025 puts Greenland's rare-earth reserves at 1.5 million tonnes of rare-earth oxide — about 1.7% of a world total of more than 90 million tonnes — with no mine production in 2023 or 2024. That is eighth in the USGS table, just behind the United States (1.9 million tonnes); China holds 44 million and Brazil 21 million, and China's Shenghe Resources has a 6.5% stake in the unmined Kvanefjeld deposit. On the ground, nothing has changed. No construction has started, no budget line published, no notices for new sites. Trump said a large presence would begin immediately; reports mention three new bases in southern Greenland, expanded radar for Russian aircraft, more support for submarine-hunting in the GIUK Gap, and sensors or interceptors linked by the administration to its planned Golden Dome missile-defense system. The physical delta today is zero, and the Golden Dome link is administration-linked, not in joint text. "NO COST" also faces a filter that has not been cleared: building Arctic bases through ice, darkness and long supply lines, likely via a deep-water port, plus radar upgrades and Golden Dome integration, will require US appropriations. No costs have been disclosed.

And permanence is not self-executing. In Denmark, Section 19 of the Constitution requires consent of the Folketing, the parliament, for major international commitments. In Greenland, the 2009 Act on Greenland Self-Government recognizes Greenlanders as a people with the right to self-determination, gives Greenland's 31-seat parliament, the Inatsisartut, authority over transferred fields including resources, and requires its opinion on international agreements affecting Greenland. In the US, a treaty needs two-thirds of the Senate, and even an executive agreement needs Congress to fund bases and authorize operations. No Folketing or Inatsisartut vote had occurred as of Sept. 19-20, and no post-Sept. 18 poll of Greenlanders has been published. Both Danish and Greenlandic releases said the agreement will enter into force subject to parliamentary procedures. Senate Arctic Caucus co-chair Lisa Murkowski said respect for Greenlanders' sovereignty should be non-negotiable; other senators warned tariff coercion and earlier talk of not ruling out military options, later walked back, would shatter allied trust.

Greenland is not just geography. The world's largest island, home to about 57,000 people, majority Inuit, self-governing within the Kingdom of Denmark since 2009 with control of natural resources and a legal path to independence by referendum, it still depends on fishing and a Danish block grant of about $511 million — roughly 20% of GDP and more than half the public budget — a dependency any investment provisions could alter. Its own 2024 foreign, defense and security strategy, titled "Nothing About Us Without Us," prioritized preventing further militarization and making the Arctic a zone of peace.

Politics reflects that tension. On Jan. 10 all five parties in the Inatsisartut said jointly: "We do not want to be Americans, we do not want to be Danes, we want to be Greenlanders," adding no other country can meddle and Greenland must decide its future. The party Naleraq, with 8 of 31 seats, diverges by advocating direct Greenland-US talks and a free-association model. Dissent is concrete. In Narsarsuaq, a former US base site in the south now discussed for a return, residents told Greenland's public broadcaster KNR on Sept. 19 they fear displacement if a base returns, noting US Navy personnel surveying the runway. In the Folketing, Greenlandic MP Aaja Chemnitz Larsen of Inuit Ataqatigiit called the framework "insane" and said neither Trump nor NATO has a mandate to negotiate Greenland's fate without Greenlanders. These are illustrative, not representative of 57,000 people, but they show why executive confirmations without a vote overstate consent. A January 2025 Verian/Berlingske poll found 85% opposed joining the US and 6% in favor, while 56% would vote for independence from Denmark — pre-deal sentiment, with no post-announcement measure. A reported clause that the agreement would survive independence would bind a future independent Greenland, directly intersecting the 2009 Act's referendum path. Whether that is compatible with self-determination, and whether the Inatsisartut would consent, is unresolved.

Why Washington wants it is clear, even if often inflated. Pituffik's radar geometry and GIUK Gap coverage matter for missile warning and tracking Russia's Northern Fleet on the Kola Peninsula, though analysts see little evidence of imminent Russian or Chinese encirclement around Greenland; Russia has reactivated Arctic bases and keeps icebreakers. On minerals, Greenland holds about 1.5 million tonnes of rare-earth reserves — about 1.7% of world reserves, similar to US reserves and far behind China's 44 million — and no rare-earth mining has yet taken place; the Kvanefjeld deposit was banned in 2022 over uranium concerns, and a 6.5% Chinese stake there has not produced a realized project. Airport proposals involving Chinese capital were blocked in 2018 under US and Danish pressure. On sea lanes, Arctic sea ice has retreated sharply, opening Northern Sea Route and Northwest Passage on paper, but retreat does not equal traffic. Beijing declared itself a "near-Arctic state" in 2018 and operates icebreakers but lacks capability for distant Arctic military presence.

The second-order stakes are about precedent. Even if little is built, a permanent, no-cost, post-independence security encumbrance on a self-governing people's territory sets a template others could cite. It also teaches an alliance lesson: pressure — tariff threats and earlier hints at military options — can extract formalized exclusivity without a sovereignty transfer.

The competing steelmen are therefore both grounded. For the administration: codify Arctic exclusivity, foreclose a Panama-style commercial foothold, secure missile-warning geometry and GIUK surveillance, and keep NATO cohesion. For Denmark and Greenland: a binding framework that strengthens common security, benefits NATO and Europe, and affirms sovereignty and self-determination while giving Greenland leverage and a security umbrella. For Greenlandic dissent: legitimacy requires free, prior and informed consent of a people, not just signatures. For parliaments: permanence must survive votes and budgets. For analysts: the deal may change little physically while changing the order.

What would show which view is right is observable: publication of text and annexes, Folketing and Inatsisartut votes, US Defense Department budget lines for Pituffik and any southern sites, construction contracts and notices, and Danish-Greenlandic investment-screening legislation. Absence of those in the next 6-12 months would support the "changes little" view; their appearance would support the "changes the order" view. Until then the reader holds a framework with two descriptions, a sweeping baseline that already favored the US, and gates that have not yet opened.
