China has built two new antenna compounds since mid-2024 at its only acknowledged overseas base in Djibouti, a September 17, 2026 review of commercial satellite images by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, a Washington research group, found. The arrays — high-frequency radio antennas, very-high and ultra-high frequency antennas, satellite dishes, likely 5G towers and two domed radomes that hide what is inside — very likely form a single system for command and communications and would almost certainly be able to support eavesdropping on nearby forces if tasked. Whether they are actually intercepting anything, what is inside the radomes, and whether any data is shared onward cannot be confirmed from images alone.

That distinction — hardware confirmed, active collection unproven — is the core finding. CSIS, using Planet Labs and Vantor imagery, describes the two compounds at the People's Liberation Army Support Base at Doraleh, near Djibouti City, as linked halves of one architecture. Each centers on a control building with a mix of antennas covering a wide range of frequencies and reinforced cable trenches laid in 2025 that suggest more antennas to come. CSIS frames it as redundant long-distance radio and satellite links to Chinese ships and aircraft and a secure link back to headquarters in China. The base's 341-meter quay, finished in 2022, and its reported capacity of a few thousand personnel are older infrastructure, not part of this 2024-2026 build.

Geography gives the hardware meaning. The base sits at the Bab el-Mandeb, the roughly 20-mile strait — the Gate of Tears — that connects the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden. About a sixth of global maritime trade passes there, by CSIS's estimate; other estimates put it at roughly a tenth of seaborne oil and a quarter to a third of container traffic, making it the third-busiest oil chokepoint after Hormuz and Malacca.

Djibouti, a small state in the Horn of Africa, has made a business of that location, hosting five foreign militaries within about 10 miles of each other to collect rent and security guarantees while balancing powers. Within that radius of China's base sit the United States' Camp Lemonnier — the only permanent U.S. base in Africa, home to more than 4,000 personnel — plus French, Italian and Japanese facilities. Japan resumed maritime patrol flights from Djibouti in August 2026. The U.S. Air Force describes a nearby airfield as the only site in East Africa authorized for armed surveillance aircraft. In short, a dense cluster of allied sensors and transmitters operates within line of sight of a new Chinese antenna farm.

The same antennas can do two jobs, and readings diverge there.

The established function is backup communications. Long-distance high-frequency radio and satellite links give the Chinese navy, which has kept rotating escort groups in the Gulf of Aden since 2008, another way to talk to ships, aircraft and China when other channels are degraded.

The second, likely function is signals intelligence — picking up electronic emissions. CSIS assesses the varied type and orientation of the new equipment would almost certainly support such a mission and likely enable monitoring of nearby militaries. Even when content is encrypted, the pattern reveals activity: where a signal comes from, where it goes, on what frequency and how often.

But CSIS explicitly cautions that open-source imagery cannot confirm whether passive collection is actually underway. The Maritime Executive, in an independent review of the same imagery, adds a physical limit: from low-lying Doraleh, long-range very-high frequency interception is height-limited, while high-frequency interception — which regional militaries still use for long distance — is practical. The site is well placed to hear some bands and poorly placed for others, and no public data shows which, if any, are being collected.

That leaves a counter-reading that fits the same hardware. Analysts at Defense Priorities and other independent reviewers note the Djibouti base remains limited for power projection — a short runway that cannot take fighters or large transports, no helicopters, and, according to a Pentagon report to Congress in December 2025, minimal role in protecting Chinese citizens or in the Red Sea crisis. On that view, a full-spectrum antenna farm is equally consistent with sustaining far-seas escort operations and securing communications, not proof of a dedicated spy station. Capability does not equal intent.

Timing raises the stakes without proving causation. Since late 2023, Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping and U.S. and Israeli operations against Iran have crowded the region with collectable emissions. By July 2026, more than 20 U.S. Navy warships, including two carrier strike groups, plus hundreds of aircraft were operating in the Arabian Sea and around Hormuz, according to U.S. notices.

The Houthis, the Yemeni armed movement Ansar Allah, declared on July 20 a blockade on Saudi Arabia, saying they would target Saudi, Israeli and U.S.-linked tankers in the Bab el-Mandeb. Shipping data showed transits dipped about a quarter the following week then steadied — a shock followed by adaptation, not collapse. Hormuz remains the far larger oil chokepoint; Bab el-Mandeb matters as the broader trade chokepoint across containers, bulk and energy. A collector at Djibouti that can characterize activity at one gains value from activity at the other, and the surge means there is more to characterize.

Three tiers need to stay separate: confirmed hardware exists; it is likely capable of eavesdropping and would almost certainly support it if tasked; whether it is actively intercepting, which bands and targets, what is inside the two radomes, and whether data is shared onward — for example to Iran or the Houthis, as the Maritime Executive speculates — is unproven.

No on-record assessment of the September upgrade has appeared from the headquarters that would know most: U.S. Africa Command and Camp Lemonnier, French forces in Djibouti, Japan's defense ministry, or the Djiboutian government, which elevated ties with China to a comprehensive strategic partnership in September 2024. China's foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jian said when asked that he was unaware of the specific situation, adding that China is a builder of peace that upholds mutual respect and plays a constructive role in regional stability. No Chinese military disclosure of purpose, cost, contractor or precise timeline beyond "since mid-2024" has appeared.

How much any collector would actually learn is also undisclosed. U.S. and allied forces routinely assume they are within earshot and practice emissions control — radio silence, shielded equipment, frequency hopping — but no public disclosure details specific mitigations for this upgrade, leaving net gain uncertain. Prior friction — lasers used against U.S. flights and a 2024 Chinese attempt to restrict airspace over the base — shows awareness of proximity, not effectiveness.

The wider footprint is context, not center. Djibouti was meant to be the first of several overseas bases; other sites have emerged more as access points for visiting ships, and the navy now leans on commercial ports while keeping a rotating escort group at Djibouti. The upgrade thus functions as a persistent communications and intelligence node without new bases.

What would change the assessment is concrete: identification of radome payloads, signal-activity data, or an allied disclosure about emissions control. Until then, the picture is hardware confirmed, capability likely, collection unproven — at a chokepoint where even the metadata of a crowded sea has become more valuable.
