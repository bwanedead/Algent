The Colorado River's two giant reservoirs — Lake Powell behind Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead behind Hoover Dam — fell to their lowest levels on record in late summer 2026. Powell dropped to 3,517 feet on Sept. 10, below its April 2023 low; Mead sank to 1,039 feet at the end of August, its lowest since it first filled in 1935. Together they hold 92% of the basin's stored water — the supply for 40 million people, power for about a million homes and irrigation for 5 million acres across seven US states and Mexico.

### A system one-third full

Powell at 3,517 feet is about 183 feet below full and held about 5.1 million acre-feet at the end of August, about 22% of what it can usefully hold. An acre-foot — water covering an acre a foot deep, about 326,000 gallons — is the basin's accounting unit, roughly what two to three households use in a year.

The whole basin started water year 2026 with 21.8 million acre-feet, 37% of its 58.5-million capacity, down 3.35 million from a year earlier. By mid-July Powell and Mead together held about 12.7 million acre-feet, the lowest since before Powell filled in the early 1960s. One wet winter like 2022-23 cannot refill a hole that deep.

Powell was 27 feet above the level where its turbines stop working reliably. Below 3,490 feet, Glen Canyon Dam's eight turbines — 1,320 megawatts at full capacity — can no longer generate effectively. It was 147 feet above dead pool, about 3,370 feet, where no water can pass the dam at all. Every foot of decline costs about 3 megawatts; generation has already fallen about 17%, from about 4,600 gigawatt-hours a year before the drought to about 3,800.

### Why the river shrank

The immediate trigger was winter 2025-26. Eight Western states set record lows on April 1 — when snowpack is normally deepest — for snow water equivalent, the water contained in snow. The Colorado River Basin had its warmest March on record, 13.7 degrees Fahrenheit above normal. Peak snow arrived three to five weeks early, most forecast points were expected to produce less than 30% of average spring-summer runoff, and drier soils soaked up more before it reached the river.

The Bureau of Reclamation, the federal agency that operates the dams, forecast the natural flow that would have reached Powell without dams at 3.52 million acre-feet for water year 2026, 37% of average and among the lowest on record. August inflow was about 1,000 acre-feet, effectively zero.

That bad winter sits atop a 25-year drying. The period 2000-2022 was the lowest 23-year inflow period since Glen Canyon Dam began filling in 1963, averaging 8.29 million acre-feet versus a 1991-2020 average of 9.60 million. Flows have shrunk about 20% since 2000 as warming increased evaporation and reduced how efficiently rain and snow become river flow.

How much of the 2026 snow drought was driven by human-caused warming versus natural variability is not established. Two other widely reported figures — a projection that Mead could fall to about 998 feet by August 2028, and the scale of future delivery cuts — have not been confirmed in the published federal documents themselves.

### Emergency plumbing that moves the problem

In April 2026 the Bureau began emergency operations to keep Powell above 3,490 feet. It is zero-sum, not a fix.

Three linked moves: release 660,000 to 1 million acre-feet from Flaming Gorge Reservoir on the Green River in northern Utah and southern Wyoming through April 2027, dropping it from about 83% full toward about 59%; cut annual releases from Powell to Mead from 7.48 million acre-feet to 6.0 million, a 20% cut; and cancel an April controlled flood meant to rebuild sandbars and an August release of cold deep water meant to protect native fish.

The goal is to raise Powell about 54 feet to at least 3,500 feet by April 2027. The Bureau's September outlook projects Powell could reach 3,558 feet by June 2028 if the smaller releases continue.

The trade-off is that Mead falls faster. Hoover Dam's capacity could drop about 40% as soon as this fall. Upstream states warn the shuffling only delays the underlying imbalance by one to two years without demand cuts, and insist every drop taken from Flaming Gorge be fully recovered when hydrology improves. Blue Mesa Reservoir in Colorado is already barely above its own power pool — the level below which it too cannot generate — and senior water rights dating to the 1860s face curtailment.

The canceled environmental flows are contested. The cold-water release would have drawn from deep below the warm intakes to keep invasive smallmouth bass from spawning. Below about 3,530 feet bass passage through the dam increases; without it, water in the upper 70s Fahrenheit can trigger explosive growth.

### Who gets cut

The shortage is baked into law. The 1922 Colorado River Compact split the river 7.5 million acre-feet to the Upper Basin — Colorado, Utah, Wyoming and New Mexico — and 7.5 million to the Lower Basin — Arizona, California and Nevada — plus 1.5 million to Mexico under a 1944 treaty. The split was based on flows measured during unusually wet years that have never returned.

Under that Law of the River, priority decides who is cut first. In the Lower Basin, California holds senior rights to 4.4 million acre-feet, Arizona 2.8 million and Nevada 0.3 million. Arizona's Central Arizona Project — the 336-mile canal that carries river water to Phoenix and Tucson — is junior within the Lower Basin, so it absorbs cuts first. Within that canal, tribes are senior, then cities, then non-tribal agriculture is most junior.

A 10-year Decision Framework for 2027-2036, whose Record of Decision was issued Aug. 21, 2026 to replace 2007 rules expiring this year, would reassess operations every two years. Federal planning has contemplated cuts of up to about 3 million acre-feet per year to Lower Basin deliveries on a priority basis — about 40% for those states. The Lower Basin states have offered a voluntary alternative of 1.25 million acre-feet per year for two years — Arizona 760,000, California 440,000, Nevada 50,000 — still about 27-30% of Arizona's allocation and about half the canal's supply. Seven states missed a February deadline to agree on new rules.

The same shrinking water now pulls six interests in opposite directions, and the fight is over who bears permanent cuts when the water to honor every promise is not there.

Upper Basin states, through the Upper Colorado River Commission, say natural shortage already imposes more than 40% cuts on them — more than 2.0 million acre-feet in 2026 — and that Lower Basin demand for Phoenix, Las Vegas and California agriculture drove the depletion of Powell and Mead, which the Upper Basin does not directly rely on. Chair Becky Mitchell described it as being asked "to solve a problem we didn't create with water we don't have." Lower Basin governors argue the system was over-allocated in 1922 and all basins benefited from storage assumptions, so depletion is a shared responsibility.

Tribal nations, holding senior and often unquantified rights excluded from the 1922 Compact, say guidelines that deviate from priority breach the federal trust responsibility without consent. The Gila River Indian Community — the largest canal holder with about 121,000 acre-feet — said it will challenge cuts "in every forum available" if final guidelines fall short. The Colorado River Indian Tribes, holding more than 23% of Arizona's allocation with the most senior rights, said its limited role does not reflect its rights. The Navajo Nation notes its Arizona rights remain unquantified after a settlement for the Navajo, Hopi and San Juan Southern Paiute was blocked in Congress by Upper Basin states, and the White Mountain Apache Tribe warns its 52,000 acre-foot settlement, including 25,000 acre-feet of canal water, is at risk.

Mexico, under an amendment to the treaty known as Minute 334 signed Sept. 2, 2026 in El Paso through December 2028, accepted a proportional cut of 250,000 acre-feet per year — about 17% of its 1.5-million allocation — matching the Lower Basin's 1.25-million burden, with consultation on further cuts, potential increases if Mead reaches 1,125 feet, and $5.6 million for delta habitat. Its commissioners emphasized parity, not bearing disproportionate shortage while U.S. basins argue.

Environmental groups — the Glen Canyon Institute, American Rivers and the Center for Biological Diversity — call the canceled flood and cold-water release an abandonment of humpback chub recovery and bass control, externalizing ecological costs for a one- to two-year delay. The Glen Canyon Institute called 2026 the "most predicted disaster of all time" and criticized the failure to assess retrofits for a dam whose plumbing was not designed for low water. Hydropower customers — 155 utilities with priority to buy federal hydropower, represented by the Colorado River Energy Distributors Association — and the Bureau call keeping Powell above power pool non-negotiable for the Western grid, which totals 286,000 megawatts. They called the cold-water cancellation the "right call" to avoid millions in replacement power costs.

Without permanent conservation, reservoir shuffling moves water from one low pool to another. Demand grew as supply shrank — Phoenix, Las Vegas and California agriculture expanded while the river lost about a fifth of its flow — and groundwater pumping has accelerated as surface cuts deepen. The question left for the post-2026 rules is not whether the river is smaller than the promises written for it — it is — but whether senior rights, tribal trust responsibilities and treaty parity can be honored at all.
