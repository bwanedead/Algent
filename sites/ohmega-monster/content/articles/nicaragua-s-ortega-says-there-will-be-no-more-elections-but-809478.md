Daniel Ortega said Nicaragua would no longer hold elections during a speech marking the anniversary of the Sandinista Revolution, and the National Assembly said it was beginning work on a plan to implement his directives. Those two moves are the clearest evidence so far that the ruling system is pushing beyond managed elections and toward something more final.

But the legal picture is not yet settled. Nicaragua’s electoral law, passed in 2025, still says general elections are to be held on the first Sunday after Nov. 2 of the election year. It also gives the Supreme Electoral Council power to organize and supervise elections, approve the calendar, validate candidates, authorize alliances, and cancel party legal status in some cases — the kinds of powers that can keep rivals off the ballot or leave them without a viable party. That is a highly controlled system, but it is still a system that calendars elections.

The open question is the one that matters most: has Nicaragua formally abolished the 2027 cycle, or is the regime turning it into a one-sided process in which Ortega and Rosario Murillo would run without real competition? The reporting in hand does not show the exact decree, amendment or resolution that would definitively cancel that election.

The constitutional reform adopted earlier in 2025 helps explain why the move can advance inside the state. It centralizes coordination of the legislative, judicial and electoral organs in the presidency, and it rewrites rights on expression, assembly, association and political participation so that they are conditioned on the constitution’s “fundamental principles.” In other words, the legal architecture already gave the executive and the electoral system wide room to squeeze political competition.

That context matters because Nicaragua was already deep into repression before Ortega’s announcement. The U.N. human-rights office said at least 46 people remained arbitrarily detained for political reasons, civic space was nearly closed, and independent expression was systematically suppressed. Volker Türk, the U.N. high commissioner for human rights, urged the authorities to release detainees, reopen civic space, restore the rule of law and let people of all political viewpoints vote and run for office.

The government’s own justification is part of the picture, but not proof. The National Assembly said it was drawing up its work plan to implement Ortega’s directives in the name of peace, security, stability and continuity of anti-poverty gains. Ortega himself framed elections as a mechanism for hostile forces to seize power and tied them to U.S.-backed or Somocista-linked adversaries.

Washington treated the announcement as something closer to an explicit break with electoral legitimacy. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Ortega and Murillo had “abandoned even the pretense of popular consent” and called on other governments to join forces against the Murillo-Ortega government, even as the reporting still has not shown the specific decree, amendment or resolution that would cancel the 2027 calendar.

For now, the strongest grounded reading is narrower than the rhetoric on either side. Nicaragua is clearly moving to extinguish electoral competition. What remains unknown is whether the state will write that into law as formal abolition, or preserve the machinery of elections while emptying it of competition.
