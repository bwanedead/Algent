On 8 September 2026 Google DeepMind, the London-based AI lab, released a free, browsable Atlas that predicts what every possible single-letter change in human DNA might do to gene regulation. Any researcher can now look up a variant in a browser and get a ranked hypothesis in seconds — a real shift for rare-disease and population genetics — but a high score is not a diagnosis and still needs laboratory proof.

Human DNA is about 3.2 billion letters long, written in A, C, G and T. At each position there are three possible swaps, so about 9 billion possible single-letter changes, plus short insertions or deletions. Only about 2% codes directly for proteins. The other 98% is regulatory: it controls when, where and how strongly genes are turned on. Most variants linked to common traits and diseases in large genome studies lie there, and it has been the hardest part to interpret.

The Atlas is an attempt to make that 98% searchable. DeepMind ran its sequence-to-function model across hg38, the current standard human reference genome, comparing each reference base to its three alternatives — exhaustive computer simulation — and stored the results. It covers all ~9 billion possible single-letter changes plus more than 100 million short insertions or deletions seen in large datasets such as gnomAD, UK Biobank and All of Us in the United States. Each variant carries thousands of predictions across hundreds of cell types and tissues, totalling more than 240 trillion values. At 1 petabyte it is more than 30 times larger than the AlphaFold Database as expanded in 2022 from about 190,000 experimental protein structures to more than 200 million predicted ones. The 9 billion are all possible changes, not variants already seen in patients.

The Atlas is not a new model. It is a precomputed version of AlphaGenome, DeepMind's generalist model released in 2025 and described in Nature in early 2026.

AlphaGenome takes up to 1 million letters of DNA context at once and predicts effects at single-base resolution across 11 molecular readouts — including gene expression, splicing, chromatin accessibility and 3D genome contacts — across hundreds of human and mouse cell types. It was trained on data from four public consortia that catalogue gene regulation. In that Nature evaluation it was reported as state-of-the-art on 22 of 24 track-prediction tasks and 25 of 26 variant-effect tasks, with a preprint version reporting 24 of 26. Those numbers depend on task and comparator choice and do not amount to universal superiority. DeepMind and independent researchers note overall accuracy remains below AlphaFold's protein-structure accuracy, and performance is stronger for promoters and splicing than for distant enhancers.

Making exhaustive precomputation feasible took engineering. DeepMind distilled a smaller, faster model from a slower ensemble and optimised it to run in less than a second per variant on a leading AI chip, where earlier estimates had deemed a full-genome run impossible.

The Atlas condenses those thousands of predictions into layers a biologist can scan. First, raw molecular predictions per variant. Second, a single-number rank called the AlphaGenome Variant Impact score, or AVI. AVI merges 10 aggregated regulatory signals with protein-impact scores, conservation and other annotations — 18 features in total — trained to distinguish rare from common variants as a proxy for impact. It is scored like the PHRED scale used in genetics: 10 means top 10% most impactful genome-wide, 20 top 1%, 30 top 0.1% — one in a thousand. Each AVI comes with attributions showing which process drives it, such as splicing or accessibility. Third, those attributions make the score inspectable. Fourth, a compendium of 2,601 recurrent short DNA words — including sites where regulatory proteins bind — mapped across the genome, some matching known biology, others labelled broad, unresolved or unknown and not all validated. The Atlas is locked to hg38 and does not update in real time for new cell types or a future reference without recomputation.

Before Atlas, using AlphaGenome meant calling an interface and running graphics-processor compute, which capped users at about 9,000 globally. Atlas removes that friction with a free web portal requiring no coding, plus interface access and an agent-assisted workflow. EMBL-EBI, the European bioinformatics institute that co-runs Ensembl, is embedding AVI scores into Ensembl's Variant Effect Predictor, the widely used annotation tool many clinical pipelines already run.

Access is free for non-commercial academic research. Commercial access via Google Cloud is described only as coming soon, with no disclosed launch date, price, licensing terms, rate limits or bulk-download costs for the 1-petabyte dataset. DeepMind's sister drug-discovery company Isomorphic Labs will also require a commercial licence. That creates a two-tier structure unlike AlphaFold DB's fully open release, with risks of reference-genome bias, staleness and asymmetry between well-funded companies and academic labs. No ancestry breakdown or false-positive rate for AVI was disclosed in the launch materials.

DeepMind highlighted two retrospective pilots selected by its collaborators, not prospective trials. They illustrate hypothesis generation, not proven diagnostic yield.

*What the Atlas changes for finding causes in the 98% non-coding genome — and what it still does not do. AlphaGenome Atlas improved two incumbent filters, and still left most rare-disease causes unfound. In retrospective tests on solved GREGoR cases, AVI placed the known causal variant in the top 50 candidates 29.5% of the time versus 12.5% for CADD (about 2.4×), which means 70.5% of those cases still sat outside the top 50. Grouping rare variants by Atlas-predicted effect in 54,189 UK Biobank genomes across 2,028 proteins found 728 significant non-coding associations versus 595 by the standard method (+22%, +133), including a PLA2G7 locus narrowed from 526 candidates to 4 — both tests are reported, retrospective, and not a prospective diagnostic yield; Atlas is not approved or validated for clinical use. — AI-assisted analytic, built only from real cited or sourced data. Source: Fortune, TechTimes, WinBuzzer. As of 2026-09-09.*

At the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard in the United States, researchers Laura Covill and Anne O'Donnell-Luria working with the NIH-funded GREGoR Consortium for unsolved rare diseases used AVI to reprioritize variants that conventional filters had overlooked. The top hit was a deep intronic variant in DNM1, a gene strongly linked to epileptic encephalopathy. AlphaGenome predicted it creates a brain-specific cryptic splice site that extends the protein by 13 amino acids. Because that brain form is barely expressed in blood, earlier RNA sequencing from blood had been inconclusive. A laboratory minigene assay — a cell-based test of splicing — across five cell lines reproduced the predicted effect, and the variant was reclassified as likely pathogenic.

In retrospective testing on solved GREGoR cases, AVI placed the known causal variant in the top 50 candidates 29.5% of the time versus 12.5% for CADD, the widely used genome-wide ranker — about a 2.4-fold improvement. That figure is retrospective on solved cases selected by DeepMind collaborators, with no independent replication outside those cases, no prospective diagnostic yield, and no CADD version specified. It should be read as illustrative prioritization recall, not the rate at which unsolved patients would now be solved.

At the University of Exeter in the United Kingdom, Medical Research Council fellow Gareth Hawkes applied Atlas to whole-genome data from more than 54,000 UK Biobank participants — 54,189 in the preprint analysis across 2,028 circulating proteins. By grouping rare non-coding variants by their predicted molecular effect, the analysis found 728 significant non-coding rare-variant associations with protein levels that remained distinct after overlapping signals were separated, up from 595 without those aggregated features — 22% more signals under that specific grouping method. Examples included PLA2G7, linked to aging, and EGLN1, a cellular oxygen sensor, and in one locus the approach narrowed 526 candidates to four. A separate body-mass-index analysis identified 19 associated regions after focusing on the top 1% most impactful non-coding variants. Those are additional association signals in that cohort and method; they measure neither predictive accuracy nor causation, and the supporting research remains a preprint without peer review.

DeepMind states explicitly that Atlas is not approved or validated for clinical use and is not a substitute for professional medical advice; predictions are hypotheses requiring independent laboratory validation.

That caution is built into clinical genetics rules. Under the 2015 joint guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology in the US, computational predictor evidence is capped at supporting-level only, with an explicit warning not to overestimate it because different algorithms may rely on similar data and most have not been validated against well-established pathogenic variants. If predictions disagree, the evidence cannot be counted as supporting.

AVI has no published calibration to exceed that ceiling. The ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group, which calibrates how strongly a tool can be counted, found in 2022 and expanded in 2025 that only 4 of 13 evaluated tools could reach strong support, nine topped out at moderate or supporting, and one failed to meet even supporting. CADD's developer-recommended threshold of 20 falls within moderate for benign evidence, meaning it has commonly been misapplied as pathogenic evidence. REVEL, another widely used predictor, is the only tool covering strong to very strong at specific thresholds. AVI, trained on an allele-frequency threshold as a rarity proxy, is not among ClinGen-calibrated tools and has no published thresholds; clinical labs therefore cannot apply it above supporting without formal calibration. A low AVI score likewise must not falsely reassure.

Other limits are structural. The 1-million-letter window misses enhancers acting over longer distances, single-variant scores ignore interactions between multiple variants and environmental or developmental context, and variant-effect benchmarks can be inflated when the same variant or same gene appears in both training and test data. AVI's hybrid design inherits both power and that leakage risk, and the 22/24 versus 25/26 versus 24/26 discrepancies reflect task and comparator choice.

The practical shift is a bottleneck move. Before Atlas the limiting factor was compute — which variants to test and how to run the model. After Atlas the limiting factor is experimental validation capacity: labs can only follow up a small number of candidates in cells or animals.

That makes Atlas infrastructure, not an answer. It collapses the cost of filtering the non-coding genome the way AlphaFold in 2020 — recognised with a share of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — collapsed the cost of getting a protein shape and AlphaMissense in 2023 collapsed coding missense scoring for 71 million variants. The Human Genome Project in 2003, in this telling, bought the book without knowing how to read it.

What to watch is whether that filter translates into real prospective yield: Ensembl integration going live, Google Cloud commercial terms and bulk-access pricing, independent replication beyond the two DeepMind-selected vignettes, formal ClinGen calibration of AVI thresholds, and a future model that sees beyond 1 million letters to capture distal enhancers and multi-variant effects. Until then, the Atlas is best understood as a searchable dictionary for non-coding DNA — powerful for generating the next experiment, not for settling the diagnosis on its own.
