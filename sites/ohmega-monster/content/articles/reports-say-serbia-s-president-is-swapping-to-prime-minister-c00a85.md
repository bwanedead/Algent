Serbia's president was reported on 27 September to have resigned to run for prime minister — a legal way to stay in power after hitting his term limit.

Aleksandar Vucic — president since 2017 and the country's dominant politician since 2014 — was reported to have stepped down to lead his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) in a snap parliamentary election reported for 25 October, with parliament Speaker Ana Brnabic designated as likely acting president until a presidential vote due within 90 days, by late December.

That calendar is still reported, not confirmed. As of 28 September it rested on a single wire report republished via the Frontier Post and a Wikipedia update. No resignation decree had appeared in Serbia's Official Gazette, where laws and appointments take legal effect, no statement on the Presidency website, and no calendar from the Republic Electoral Commission, which runs elections. No reaction had appeared on the primary sites of the EU's enlargement department, the EU's foreign service, the US Embassy in Belgrade, or Kosovo's government either. Until the Gazette publishes, the dates and the vacancy remain reported.

### Why prime minister is not a step down

Serbia is a parliamentary republic with two executives. The president, directly elected for five years and limited to two terms under Article 116 of the 2006 Constitution, is head of state. The prime minister heads the Government, which the constitution calls the holder of executive power.

That split is why the reported move matters. Vucic was prime minister from 2014 to 2017, then president from 2017, winning in the first round in 2017 and 2022. His second term would otherwise end in May 2027, and Article 116 bars a third. The Law on Government sets no term limit for prime minister and no bar on a former two-term president taking the job; the incompatibility ends on resignation. If SNS wins a majority and the National Assembly elects him, Vucic would shift to direct control of government without amending the constitution.

The comparison often made to Russia's Putin-Medvedev rotation is useful only as an illustration of a dual executive where only one office is term-limited. Whether the swap violates the spirit of the constitution is disputed, not settled. No decision by Serbia's Constitutional Court and no opinion by the Venice Commission — the Council of Europe's constitutional advisory body — on this role-swap had appeared as of 28 September. "Legally permitted on the letter" is accurate; "democratically legitimate" remains contested.

The succession rule is confirmed even if the vacancy is not. Under Article 120, the Speaker serves as acting president for at most three months if the office is vacant. A secondary report described Vucic handing Brnabic the presidential seal on 28 September, but whether she actually serves depends on formal confirmation in the Gazette. The parliamentary arithmetic is fixed: 250 seats, 126 for a majority. No list composition beyond Vucic heading the SNS list had been detailed, and no presidential candidates for December had been nominated in the sources available. The parliamentary vote was reported for 25 October 2026, brought forward from a deadline of December 2027.

### Can street protests become votes?

The October test is whether a protest movement can overcome a dominant party's structural advantage.

SNS has governed since 2012 and won a parliamentary majority in 2023, while its junior partner and the main opposition coalition trailed far behind. International monitors rank Serbia as a flawed democracy to electoral autocracy, citing pressure on media, opposition and civil society under SNS dominance. Allegations of fraud in December 2023 triggered protests at the time.

The opposition has reconfigured around the student-led anti-corruption movement born from the Novi Sad railway station canopy collapse in November 2024, which killed 16 people and became a symbol of corruption. The disaster forced resignations of ministers and later the prime minister, replaced in April 2025 by Djuro Macut, nominated by Vucic. Student demands moved from disclosure of reconstruction documents to calls for snap elections. Through 2025 and into May 2026, demonstrations of 100,000 to 325,000 people were reported.

That movement is now an electoral actor as the Student List, also called Students Win, alongside the pro-Western alliance European Serbia. Whether street mobilisation translates into votes is unproven. No independent 2026 polling or seat projections were available, and 2026 electoral law changes said to address irregularities were not detailed in the sources.

On coverage, media scholars cited in a secondary report allege structural bias on national televisions despite rules requiring clearly marked electoral programming. That remains an allegation via a single outlet, with no decision by REM — Serbia's broadcast regulator — or law text available to confirm a finding.

### Two stories about the same resignation

The same reported resignation is told as two different stories, and neither has been adjudicated by a court.

The SNS and government story is honorable continuity that is legal and stabilizing. In front of the Presidency on 27 September, Vucic said he had served "faithfully, honorably and honestly" through two mandates, citing preserving peace and stability through Covid, the war in Ukraine and migrations, and maintaining Serbia as the "only country in Europe with its own political stance on Ukraine" despite pressures. SNS president Milos Vucevic framed the 25 October list as "Aleksandar Vucic — United Serbia," saying it is "not Serbia of one party or one people, it is Serbia of all citizens."

The opposition and activist story is abuse of power and fabricated justice, focused on 49 pardons Vucic issued the same day, including 12 Novi Sad activists charged with attempting to overthrow the constitutional order. The liberal opposition party Movement of Free Citizens (PSG) called the pardons an attempt to exit a "politically mounted process" used for media lynching, noting six people spent seven months in detention without a verdict on the basis of Security Intelligence Agency material whose legality the defence contested. The activists themselves rejected the pardon: the student group STAV said after more than 500 days in exile and more than seven months in detention in a "fabricated procedure constructed by authoritarian arbitrariness," the same circle that devised it now pardoned them — "We do not accept mercy... We got freedom that belonged to us from start" — while defendant Srdan Djuric said "I do not seek mercy, nor pardon, nor abolition, but justice and right to fair trial," calling the pardon an attempt for SNS actors to avoid justice. Both framings are grounded in their sources, but neither has been ruled on by the Constitutional Court or the Venice Commission. Absence of a ruling is not endorsement or condemnation.

### What Brussels and Kosovo are watching

Beyond Serbia, continuity or change matters for the European Union, which conditions Serbia's EU accession on rule-of-law reforms, and for Kosovo, whose normalization of relations with Serbia Brussels also conditions on progress.

What remains to be established is basic: whether the resignation has been formally accepted and published in the Gazette and whether the Assembly has been dissolved with an official calendar will determine if 25 October and late December are real dates or reported ones. Who SNS nominates for president and what independent polls show about whether the Student List and European Serbia can together deny SNS 126 seats will determine if the role-swap is a formality or a contest.
