Nine huge building ideas — a 2km pyramid over Tokyo Bay, a 96,000km ribbon to space, a 170km mirrored wall city in the desert — sort by how far they actually got. Only one is rising. The rest are stopped by one of three filters: a material that does not exist at scale, money and politics, or the fact that most megaprojects blow budget and schedule.

"Seriously tried" here means a real builder or state with money and power behind it: Shimizu and Obayashi, builders of much of Tokyo's skyline; Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF), the fund behind NEOM; Hong Kong's government; Jeddah Economic Company; and Oceanix with UN-Habitat, the UN housing and settlements programme, and the port city of Busan. That credential is what makes the sorting useful the next time a rendering goes viral.

## Shimizu Mega-City Pyramid — Tokyo Bay

![An architect's impression of a colossal open-lattice pyramid rising from the sea, with stacked city blocks visible inside the truss structure.](/analytics/nine-giant-building-projects-that-were-seriously-backed-and-49af97/figure_shimizu-pyramid.jpg)
Not a tower but a city as one building. Shimizu's lattice, patented in 1992 and unveiled in 2004, would stand 2,004m over Tokyo Bay — about 14 times the Great Pyramid and 2.5 times the Burj Khalifa — holding 750,000 to 1 million people in blocks hung inside an open truss.

It answers Greater Tokyo's scarcity — about 33.4 million people with little buildable land — by building over water, with a truss meant to flex in quakes and typhoons. Shimizu's own timeline, start as early as 2030 and finish around 2110, signals the status: paper only. Conventional steel and concrete are too heavy. The design needs mass-produced, defect-free carbon nanotubes and graphene at strengths lab samples cannot yet deliver.

## Obayashi Space Elevator

![An architect's impression of an ultra-thin ribbon stretching vertically from a floating ocean platform through clouds into orbit, with a small climber on the line.](/analytics/nine-giant-building-projects-that-were-seriously-backed-and-49af97/figure_space-elevator.jpg)
Obayashi, builder of Tokyo Skytree, proposed in 2012 the most extreme tensile structure: a 96,000km carbon-nanotube ribbon from a floating Earth Port on the equator to beyond geostationary orbit at 36,000km, ending in a counterweight, with climbers crawling at about 200 km/h — a week to orbit.

Obayashi's own simulation says it is feasible only if a ribbon at 150 gigapascals — a measure of pulling strength — can be made, then built over about 20 years by launching a seed ribbon and thickening it to 7,000 tons. The physics of hanging is sound; the material is not. No one can spin continuous, defect-free ribbon at kilometre scale, let alone 96,000km. A small 2018 test with two 10cm CubeSats, mini-satellites, and a 10m tether was reported at the time as the first climber movement in space.

## The Line at NEOM

![An architect's impression of two parallel mirrored walls 500m high forming a narrow linear city stretching across desert toward the sea.](/analytics/nine-giant-building-projects-that-were-seriously-backed-and-49af97/figure_the-line.jpg)
The Line is the only project here with state-scale earthworks: two parallel mirrored walls 500m high, 200m wide, running 170km inland from the Red Sea, enclosing 34 sq km for 9 million people at about 265,000 per sq km, ten times Manhattan, car-free and net-zero, inside NEOM in Saudi Arabia.

Backed by PIF, it moved from a $500bn pitch to internal estimates of $1.6tn then about $4.5tn. Large earthworks were visible in satellite images by October 2024, with press reports of a SAR 700m concrete factory — in Saudi riyals — and about 1,000 of 30,000-plus piles placed with 115,000 workers on site.

Then scale collapsed. Documents cited by press cut the 2030 target from 170km and 1.5 million people to a 2.4km segment for about 300,000. By July 2025 Saudi Arabia had asked consulting firms for a strategic review as oil prices and budget shortfalls forced reprioritization, with reports of relocating NEOM staff to Riyadh and later reports of suspended work and contractor cancellations. Engineers say the walls are buildable; the veto has been money, oil-price softness and politics. No residents have moved in.

That veto includes human cost. Saudi authorities forcibly displaced Howeitat tribe members for NEOM, demolishing villages and detaining dozens, with UN experts saying in May 2023 the evictions since January 2020 amounted to forced evictions under international law. A former intelligence officer testified he was ordered in April 2020 to clear al-Khuraybah with lethal force licensed; resident Abdul Rahim al-Huwaiti was shot dead on 13 April 2020 after refusing valuation. Inside France's EDF, most hydro-engineering staff surveyed opposed involvement, citing huge carbon costs, and the company was dropped in March 2025.

## Oceanix Busan

The most modest and most plausible floating city: three linked platforms for 12,000 people, expandable to 100,000, designed by BIG-Bjarke Ingels Group and SAMOO with Arup for Oceanix with UN-Habitat and Busan.

It extends a city of 3.4 million with bridges to land, net-zero power from solar, closed-loop water and waste, and habitat regeneration — a climate-adaptation prototype for coastal populations. Unveiled at the UN in April 2022 and slated to start in 2023 tied to Busan's Expo 2030 bid, UN-Habitat said in May 2024 it was being implemented off North Port. No full neighbourhood is operational; costs and the absence of a Korean legal framework for floating habitation and insurance keep it at prototype.

## Lantau Tomorrow Vision / Kau Yi Chau

Hong Kong's answer to the world's most unaffordable housing — 220,000 people in subdivided "coffin homes" and a 5.3-year wait for public housing — was to make land. Reclamation, making new land by filling sea with sand and rock, already used on 25% of Hong Kong's coastline, would create artificial islands for 550,000 people and a third business district.

Announced in October 2018 at HK$624bn, later HK$580bn, it was the city's most expensive infrastructure ever, with first intake planned for 2032. In September 2025 Development Secretary Bernadette Linn shelved it: no "necessary conditions" within the current term, environmental assessment halted, priority shifted to the Northern Metropolis. The blockers were fiscal — costs against HK$400bn in expected land revenue, with warnings reserves could be drained in a decade — and ecological: green groups opposed loss of Chinese white dolphin habitat, down from 188 in 2003 to three or four after airport reclamation, and massive sand mining.

## Jeddah Tower

The one actively rising: 1,008m, 167 floors on the Red Sea, 170m above the Burj Khalifa and designed by the same architect, Adrian Smith with Gordon Gill, in a tapered form to shed wind. Centrepiece of the $20bn Jeddah Economic City, it holds hotel, apartments, offices and a 660m observation deck.

After reaching about 38 floors by 2016, work stopped in 2018 for seven years when contractor Saudi Binladin Group was caught in the anti-corruption purge and financing froze. Rehired in late 2024 on a SR7.2bn ($2bn) contract, with Turner Construction managing from March 2025, it restarted in January 2025 at about a floor every three to four days, reaching 69-80 floors and 454m by late 2025, targeting 2028. The blocker was politics and cash, not physics — though pumping concrete at a kilometre still tests engineering.

## Atlantropa

German architect Herman Sörgel's Atlantropa (1928-1952) is the archetype: dam the Strait of Gibraltar with foundations crowned by a tower, plus dams at Sicily-Tunisia and the Dardanelles, to lower the Mediterranean and expose 576,000 sq km — a fifth of the sea — and generate 110,000 MW, half of Europe's needs then, over 100-150 years.

It fascinated the public for decades but was never funded, rejected by the Nazis as anti-Eurasian, and rendered obsolete by nuclear power and decolonization: Venice stranded inland, the Levant gaining land without consent, salinity and climate collapsing in a basin cut off from the Atlantic, under European control. Sörgel died in 1952 after being struck by a car; his institute lasted into the 1960s. Status: never built.

## The material that doesn't exist yet

The Japanese pyramid and the space elevator share one quantified bottleneck. The best lab carbon-nanotube fiber reported in 2026 reaches 12.5 gigapascals — about 12 times below the 150 gigapascals Obayashi assumes for a 96,000km ribbon, and only a fraction of individual tubes because defects weaken the macroscopic fiber. Theory promises about 200 gigapascals; lab reality is lower, and no one can spin defect-free continuous ribbon at kilometre scale. Builders call the designs stepping stones awaiting future materials; skeptics note there is no path today to a defect-free ribbon at that length, and for The Line, unresolved wind, heat, seismic and safety gaps underline that "awaiting material" understates the barrier.

## The iron law

Even buildable projects sit in a distribution where overrun is the norm. Bent Flyvbjerg's dataset of more than 16,000 projects in 136 countries since 1910 finds 91.5% go over budget, over schedule or both, and fewer than 1% hit time, budget and promised benefits, with mean overrun 62% and a fat tail where a subset far exceed 50%.

Altitude makes the tail legible. Global megaprojects run about $9tn a year; PIF at about $700bn would need two to six times its assets to fund NEOM at $1.6-4.5tn, while Brent crude at $61-66 sits below Saudi breakeven of $80-110, driving a large deficit in 2025. Hong Kong's reclamation cost against HK$400bn revenue sits beside a HK$100bn deficit. The base rate does not predict these projects will fail at the same rate — space and floating structures have no historical analogues — but it is the filter any new rendering should be held against.
