The Strait of Hormuz was not shown to be fully closed. What the record does show is a serious operational disruption: tanker traffic slowed on July 10, fell to multi-week lows by July 13, and Reuters later reported just six vessels transiting on Sunday, the lowest count in five weeks. CNBC, using Kpler data, reported 14 ships on Sunday, down from 37 a week earlier. Those counts are only a visible floor, not a complete tally, because Reuters reported that ships were increasingly switching off AIS transponders.

That matters because Hormuz is not just any shipping lane. The EIA says the strait averaged 20 million barrels per day in 2024, about 20% of global petroleum liquids consumption, and around one-fifth of global liquefied natural gas trade also moved through it. The same EIA page says flows had not been blocked after the recent tensions. CNBC quoted the U.S. Energy Department saying more than 8 million barrels still transited on Sunday, with total flows out of the Middle East Gulf region averaging 15 million barrels per day. So the evidence points to a chokepoint under strain, not a dead one.

Commercial behavior matched that picture. Insurance Journal reported fewer quote requests, reluctance by shipowners to commit, and marine war-risk premiums rising to roughly 2% to 6% of vessel value. That is not proof of a total blockade, but it is strong evidence that market participants were treating passage as materially riskier.

The public language around the strait was also split. Reuters quoted Trump saying Hormuz is open to commercial traffic, while Reuters also reported Iran saying it was closed until further notice after a vessel traveled an unapproved route and was struck. Defense News reported the U.S.-led Joint Maritime Information Center saying neutral transit to or from non-Iranian destinations would not be impeded, while unauthorized vessels could be intercepted, diverted, or captured.

IMO’s response points to the same middle ground. Its Hormuz page says it is closely monitoring the situation, maintaining guidance and incident information, and implementing an evacuation plan for stranded seafarers, though that plan is currently paused. In other words: the route was contested, constrained, and dangerous — but the current record does not establish a verified, sustained closure.

## How we know this

**How this piece is framed:** Treat the story as a measured operational disruption with an evidence-strength gradient: visible traffic fell sharply, insurers and shipowners acted cautiously, and JMIC/IMO guidance described constrained passage rules — while official 'open/closed' statements remained conflicting and the record still does not prove a full sustained shutdown.
