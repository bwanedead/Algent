In late September 2026 a Mars rover hit 5,000 Martian days, a Jupiter probe was due to slingshot past Earth, a new 222-metre Moon crater was confirmed and a distant ring system had visibly changed. None was a new launch. Together they show how planetary science advances by staying put and being in the right place at the right time.

The coincidence is editorial, not physical. Each runs on its own clock — a rover's decade-long climb, an eight-year cruise, a 17-year watch that caught a once-a-century impact, and a ring system that evolved in ten years — and each needs its own mechanism to make sense.

## A rover still climbing after 14 years

NASA's Curiosity has been counting time in sols — one Martian day, about 24 hours 39 minutes — since it landed in Gale Crater in August 2012. By late September 2026 that count was approaching 5,000 sols, about 14 Earth years, far beyond its prime mission.

The milestone is not just endurance. On 26 August 2026 the rover passed one kilometre of elevation gain as it climbs Mount Sharp, the layered mountain at the centre of the crater, to read Mars' water history. A NASA update in early September put the mark at 5,000 sols; the mission's pages noted sol 4,740 fell on 6 December 2025. The work is familiar: hunting rocks formed in water, drilling — 42 holes to date — and looking for organics.

Fourteen years on, Curiosity is still doing field geology, just very slowly, on another planet.

## Borrowing Earth's momentum

To reach Jupiter you can carry all the fuel, or you can borrow momentum from planets — a gravity assist, using a planet's motion to gain speed without burning much fuel.

The European Space Agency's Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer, Juice — the agency's first Large-class mission in its Cosmic Vision programme, built with contributions from NASA, Japan's JAXA and Israel's space agency to complement NASA's Europa Clipper — is doing it the second way. It launched on 14 April 2023 from Europe's Spaceport in French Guiana on an Ariane 5, then began an eight-year cruise using successive flybys before reaching Jupiter in July 2031 and later orbiting Ganymede.

The Earth pass briefed on 23 September is operationally simpler than the double flyby Juice made in 2024, so its ten instruments can point freely. Closest approach is predicted for the afternoon of 28 September, about 8,600 km over the Indian Ocean, passing over Australia shortly before. The deflection is predicted at about 20 degrees with a gain of about 3.5 km per second.

A navigation campaign opened in mid-August; only the earliest correction burn was needed and an emergency slot on the 28th was reserved but not expected to be used. The spacecraft was planned to spend the night of 27-28 September in Earth's shadow on battery power, and to use the window to calibrate instruments and measure Earth's magnetotail in coordination with Smile, the joint ESA-Chinese mission. At the time of writing the flyby was still upcoming.

## A crater caught by watching for 17 years

![A fresh bowl-shaped crater about two hundred metres across with a bright ejecta blanket and dark halo on the grey, dusty lunar surface.](/analytics/four-space-clocks-show-why-staying-put-matters-as-much-as-la-bd284a/figure_crater-mcgetchin.jpg)
With no atmosphere to burn them up, impacts hit the Moon constantly, but large ones are rare. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has been circling for more than 17 years, carrying cameras that build monthly global maps and a heat-sensing instrument called Diviner.



![A whale-sized rock made a two-football-field crater that cooled miles of Moon](/analytics/four-space-clocks-show-why-staying-put-matters-as-much-as-la-bd284a/analytic_vis_02_mcgetchin_scale.svg)

*How big McGetchin is, how far its blast fluffed the surface, and how rare it is. McGetchin is 222 metres (728 feet) wide—about two football fields—and its 10–20-metre impactor left a 7-kilometre (about 4-mile) nighttime cold spot in fluffed lunar soil. A crater this large is expected only about once every 132 years, compared with roughly 140 new craters a year at the small-crater scale; measurements are from the NASA Science account and the cited Science Advances studies — AI-assisted analytic, built only from real cited or sourced data. Source: NASA Science, NASA APOD, Science Advances. As of 2026-09-23.*

That long baseline is why a new crater named McGetchin, after Apollo-era lunar scientist Tom McGetchin, was caught at all. Image-processing specialist Robert Wagner spotted a bright spot with a dark halo on a routine comparison of before-and-after global maps, then confirmed it with narrow-angle images. The crater is 222 metres wide and about 43 metres deep.

It formed between 11 April and 22 May 2024, when a comet or asteroid 10 to 20 metres across hit. Two papers in Science Advances on 16 September 2026 reported it. One documents the crater; the other reports what Diviner saw: a cold spot about seven kilometres wide, 8 to 9 kelvin cooler at night than its surroundings. The impact fluffed the upper regolith, reducing its density so it holds less heat, an anomaly extending more than 1,000 crater radii from the rim. Cold spots like this fade as the soil compacts again, on timescales of about 100,000 to two million years.

How rare is it? The orbiter has found at least 1,000 new craters and flagged more than 100,000 surface changes; at about nine metres wide, about 140 new craters form per year. McGetchin is two orders of magnitude rarer. The papers' model predicts a crater this size forms on average about once every 132 years; NASA's feature hedges that as "about once in a century or even longer."

How big a record is it? NASA's headline called it the "largest, newly formed crater ever found in the solar system" without qualification. The Science Advances paper is careful: the largest contemporary impact crater discovered anywhere in the solar system — meaning the largest new crater instrumentally observed in the space-age monitoring era, with the orbiter's monthly global imaging since 2010 and, for comparison, NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter since 2006. Larger ancient craters exist, and undetected contemporary craters could exist outside monitored areas and times. The current Mars comparator supports the qualified claim: the two largest fresh Martian craters found in 16 years, both formed in late 2021, are more than 130 metres across with the largest about 150 metres — still smaller than McGetchin's 222 metres — but that does not prove no larger new crater ever formed elsewhere unobserved. The honest ceiling is "largest discovered in the modern watch," not "largest ever formed."

The practical stake is Artemis, NASA's programme to return astronauts to the Moon. If a single impact can modify regolith miles beyond its rim, that changes rover trafficability and landing-site properties, and the orbiter's data is the map future lunar activity will rely on.

## Rings that changed in a decade

![A small dark icy body about 250 kilometres across encircled by two thin, close-spaced rings in black space.](/analytics/four-space-clocks-show-why-staying-put-matters-as-much-as-la-bd284a/figure_chariklo-rings.jpg)
Before 2013, rings were thought to belong only to giant planets. Then a stellar occultation — watching a star blink as a distant object passes in front of it — revealed that Chariklo, a small dark body about 250 km across orbiting unstably between Saturn and Uranus, has rings at all.

Chariklo is a Centaur, a small icy body on an unstable orbit between the giant planets. Its system is delicate: two narrow rings only a few kilometres wide, called C1R and C2R, circling at 390 and 405 kilometres from its centre.

A new occultation observed with the James Webb Space Telescope by Pablo Santos-Sanz and colleagues found that system has changed on a roughly ten-year timescale. Compared with visible-light observations from 2013, the inner dense ring C1R had become significantly more opaque in Webb's near-infrared view, while the outer ring C2R showed a much weaker signature, nearly vanished.

Why is unknown. The study reports the change; it does not settle the cause. Hypotheses in the field and the possibility that the difference is partly wavelength-dependent opacity rather than material loss are not mutually exclusive, and the authors' quoted line is "We don't know exactly why." Small-body rings, also seen at Haumea and Quaoar, pose a puzzle of confinement and longevity, and Chariklo shows they can evolve visibly in a decade. Detailed measurements of optical depth and width were not yet published in detail, so this account stays at the level the reporting supports.

## Why staying matters

None of these is a new launch. Curiosity is part of NASA's long-running Mars Exploration Program alongside Perseverance; Juice is an eight-year cruise that reuses Earth itself for navigation and calibration; the lunar orbiter's Diviner, built to map temperature, was repurposed for impact physics; Webb, built to see the early universe, was repurposed to time a star blink behind a 250-km rock.

That reuse is the through-line. Planetary science advances not only when a new spacecraft leaves Earth, but when an old one keeps climbing, when a trajectory borrows a planet's momentum at the right hour, when a 17-year archive lets a bright spot become a dated crater, and when a sensitive telescope is pointed at the right star at the right second. The clocks are different — sols, years, centuries, decades — but the method is shared: stay long enough, and be in the right place at the right time.
