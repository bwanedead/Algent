The Strait of Hormuz is not closed, but it is no longer operating normally.

CENTCOM says it has carried out repeated strike waves against Iran, including strikes it says were meant to degrade Iran’s ability to attack commercial shipping. Reuters reported traffic through Hormuz fell to multi-week lows, and the IMO’s confirmed-incidents log shows multiple damaged vessels and seafarer fatalities in the region in early and mid-July. That is not just rhetoric. It is active maritime impairment.

Hormuz matters because it is a chokepoint: a narrow passage through which a large share of traffic must move, so disruption there can have outsized effects. But importance is not proof of closure. The evidence here supports a narrower reading: the corridor is under stress, not definitively shut. Reuters reported Iran saying passage was not possible and its Guards saying the strait was closed, while U.S. officials said Hormuz remained open to commercial traffic. CENTCOM also said commercial transits continued and that more than 800 commercial vessels and 400 million barrels of crude—by the date of the CENTCOM report, counting transits since early May—had moved through since early May. Those claims cannot all describe the same state of affairs, which is why the best reading is contested control, not settled blockade.

The traffic drop may still be real without amounting to a full shutdown. Reuters said traffic continued at reduced levels and that operator caution was helping drive the pattern. Reuters-quoted shipping officials described a “managed conflict,” meaning shipping continues but operators deliberately adjust routes and schedules to reduce exposure to attacks, and CNBC cited shipping-industry views that the middle lane was too dangerous because of mine risk, with firms showing different risk appetites. Ships can keep moving while also deciding to move less, move differently, or move with AIS (the ship-tracking broadcast that lets others see a vessel’s location) switched off. That is compatible with precautionary routing, not just enforced closure.

The market lane is real, but only partly verified here. CNBC reported expectations that war-risk premiums would rise sharply and that shipowners and charterers were pausing transit decisions. It also reported tanker traffic had fallen and that shipping firms were reassessing routes. What this run did not verify directly is the precise live premium level or a clean market sheet. So the honest statement is that risk pricing appears to be moving, while the magnitude remains unproven here.

The tail risk is larger: a durable closure or broader regional escalation. That remains a scenario, not a demonstrated fact. For now, the reader should hold the spectrum, not a binary: confirmed impairment, continued though reduced movement, official claims that conflict, and a still-unproven leap to full blockade.
