Satyress is a California robotics startup with an official website, and the site says its robot is called Threehalves.

The company describes Threehalves as a “superhumanoid general purpose robot” with a centaur chassis. It also says the machine is meant for dangerous work in extreme environments and relies on teleoperation rather than full autonomy. That puts it in the orbit of rescue robotics: machines built for places people cannot safely enter, including disaster zones. But that is still an intended-use claim, not proof that the robot has done the job.

The site goes further than a generic teaser. It says the robot’s limbs, torso, and head can be replaced and recalibrated in minutes if damaged, using common fasteners. It says Threehalves includes fail-on air brakes and pin locks, and that shared pressure reservoirs can be pierced to stop it in place. It also gives blunt limits: the robot will be substantially slowed at a standard 32-by-80-inch door and cannot turn around in a 42-inch hallway.

Secondary coverage repeats some of the same specifics, including a two-meter height claim, remote joystick control, and modular tools such as a chainsaw, drill, or screwdriver. One report also said the price and release date were not yet known. Brett Winton’s X post, by contrast, is not a technical source: when deep-read, it only says he likes that people are thinking outside the box in terms of form factor, but he has trouble conceptualizing the strategic logic behind this particular design choice.

The evidentiary gap is still the point. The recovered sources support a real company, a named product, and a detailed design story. They do not establish field deployment, validated disaster-zone performance, autonomy behavior, payload, or readiness. So the strongest defensible reading is not that Satyress has shown a rescue robot working in the wild, but that it has made a specific claim about one.

A broader robotics reading is possible, but only conditionally. If centaur-shaped machines prove useful in hard environments, they would fit a wider trend toward specialized rescue hardware. This record does not show that Satyress is already an important case in that trend.
