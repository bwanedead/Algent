On 21 September in Dubai, the Maldives signed commercial terms handing about half its newest artificial city to Abu Dhabi developer Eagle Hills. The parcel is 500 hectares at Ras Malé — a 1,150-hectare platform being dredged at Fushi Dhiggaru Lagoon, about 17 minutes by speedboat from Malé, the capital of an Indian Ocean archipelago of about 1,200 islands and 520,000 people.

Malé is one of the world's densest cities. Ras Malé is masterplanned for about 65,000 homes, pitched as housing and climate adaptation in a country where about 80% of islands sit less than a metre above sea level. The Eagle Hills parcel alone is roughly two and a half times Downtown Dubai. What is being built on is not just sand but reef and lagoon.

![An architect's impression of a vast artificial island city built on reclaimed land in a tropical lagoon, with dense housing blocks and new shoreline.](/analytics/maldives-bets-half-a-new-artificial-island-on-a-12bn-20bn-wa-21a033/figure_ras-male-island-city.jpg)
What was signed is a terms sheet — a shared vision, not a final investment contract. The builder calls it a $12bn investment; the government calls the same project $20bn. A wider $30bn gross figure that adds future buyer money and resales has also circulated, without a published method. None is yet committed spend. The masterplan and detailed provisions are due in a definitive agreement by end-October, which will have to say how much is Eagle Hills' own equity and how much must still be found from debt and from selling flats before they are built.

In practice the project would be buyer-funded before it is built. Buyers pay before completion — so-called off-plan sales — with money held in escrow accounts in Maldivian banks and released as construction milestones are met. The state says it will take 10% of commercial sales plus 4% on every transaction including resales, with no tax holidays and all funds routed through Maldivian banks. A separate sweetener — 5,000 contractor-financed homes in Hulhumalé, paid only after completion from sale proceeds — is part of the same package.

Foreign buyers do not get freehold. Under Maldivian law they get a 99-year leasehold via a Maldives-registered entity, with a new 99-year term starting on each sale or inheritance. The constitution reserves freehold for the state and citizens, so the renewable lease is the workaround — and the point of contention. Opposition figures ask whether perpetual renewal effectively alienates sovereign land, what the land is worth beyond the 10% plus 4% fees, and what it means to lease a city in 500-hectare blocks.

Eagle Hills is the Abu Dhabi developer founded by Mohamed Alabbar, who also founded Emaar, the Dubai company behind the Burj Khalifa and Dubai Mall. The Dubai playbook is familiar: branded residences that command about a 30% premium in Dubai, sold off-plan to wealthy buyers who hold multiple properties in Dubai, London or Singapore, with escrow through local banks shifting construction risk to buyer demand. The track record is mixed: Belgrade Waterfront, 68% owned by Eagle Hills, has delivered 34 of 60 planned buildings; a marina in Albania is ongoing; a Budapest project was dropped in February 2025. The government says build-out will take 5-6 years if sales are strong and no more than 10 years if weak; Alabbar has said projects this size take 10-15 years.

Even the lower figure dwarfs the economy. Against recent IMF and World Bank estimates of about $7.1bn-$7.5bn, $12bn is about 1.6-1.7 times GDP and $20bn about 2.7-2.8 times. Together with a separate $8.8bn pledge for a Maldives International Financial Centre in Malé, also backed by Dubai-based MBS Global Investments, the two Gulf pledges each exceed GDP.

That scale lands on a balance sheet already under strain. Public and publicly guaranteed debt was about 130% of GDP in 2025 and is projected to exceed 140%, with the IMF and World Bank assessing Maldives at high risk of debt distress — meaning thresholds are breached and there is no buffer for shocks, not that default is imminent. External debt service was $630m in 2025 and spikes to about $1.7bn in 2026; after a $500m Islamic bond and a $400m Reserve Bank of India swap were settled in April 2026, about $1bn still falls due this year. Official reserves recovered to $1.3bn in March 2026 from $371m in September 2024, then fell to about $718m in April after those repayments. Usable reserves — what is left after short-term foreign-currency liabilities — were under $250m on two standard measures, less than one month of imports. The current-account deficit — the gap between what the country earns abroad and spends — is projected to widen to about 20% of GDP in 2026, and a parallel market premium of 15-20% over the official peg of 15.42 rufiyaa to the dollar has persisted despite rules forcing resorts to convert dollars locally. Fitch kept Maldives at CC on 12 June 2025 — very high default risk — and Moody's affirmed Caa2, also junk, in November 2025. Both had downgraded twice in 2024.

Tourism, the engine that must help pay for this, is about 21% of GDP directly and up to 68% with indirect contributions, providing more than 60% of foreign exchange. Arrivals hit records — 2.05 million in 2024 and 2.25 million in 2025 — but stays are shortening and spending per tourist is moderating, with arrivals down more than 20% in March-April 2026 on Middle East airspace closures. The waterfront pitch is to stretch that model from nights to months: branded second homes and marina living for high-net-worth buyers. At maturity the government and developer project more than 54,000 jobs, more than one million visitors a year and more than $2bn in annual tourism revenue. Those are preliminary estimates, not verified forecasts, with ghost-town risk if absorption is weak and pressure on water, waste and transport if it is strong.

![An architect's impression of a modern marina waterfront with mid-rise branded residences, promenades and yachts moored along the new shoreline.](/analytics/maldives-bets-half-a-new-artificial-island-on-a-12bn-20bn-wa-21a033/figure_waterfront-marina-residences.jpg)
The steelman for the government is straightforward: 65,000 homes without sovereign borrowing, billions routed through local banks, and direct revenue on every sale and resale. The steelman for skeptics is that "no sovereign guarantee" does not mean no sovereign risk. Risk transmits through foreign-currency liquidity and the bank-sovereign loop even without a guarantee — via that parallel premium, thin usable reserves, a 20% external gap, and banks' heavy exposure to the state constraining private credit. Rating agencies warn on that loop even as tourism grows. Parliamentary opposition — the Maldivian Democratic Party and People's National Front — demanded a probe and impeachment ahead of April 2024 polls after leaked Financial Intelligence Unit and police reports circa 2018 alleged irregular transfers linked to President Mohamed Muizzu, with former vice-president Mohamed Jameel Ahmed specifically alleging corruption in Ras Malé; the claims are contested and unproven.

Ras Malé is the country's largest reclamation, framed as housing necessity. Eagle Hills says its 500-hectare parcel is already reclaimed, with no further dredging and with independent marine monitoring, and has promised to respect turtles, fish and corals. That claim does not cover the lagoon. The environmental impact assessment for the full 1,150 hectares models sediment plumes to 5km and includes a coral relocation plan, conditioned on turbidity curtains and real-time monitoring. Independent measurement of similar dredging has found plumes of 3-8km, exceeding predictions. History counsels caution: Human Rights Watch documented that prior reclamations buried 70% of Kulhudhuffushi's mangroves for an airport, destroyed reefs and harmed fisheries, with assessments often done after contracts were signed. The Environmental Protection Agency, now the Environmental Regulatory Authority, has six staff for more than 200 active assessments and no public compliance database, according to that review. That thin oversight was tested during a mass bleaching event when the agency ordered dredging, reclamation and sand pumping suspended from 9 May to 10 June 2024.

For Muizzu, who campaigned on "India Out" — seeking withdrawal of about 75 Indian troops, later replaced by civilians — and then upgraded ties with China to a comprehensive strategic cooperative partnership in January 2024, the United Arab Emirates offers a transactional third pole. India remains the traditional security and development partner: ties were reset in October 2024 to a Comprehensive Economic and Maritime Security Partnership, with about $760m in support including the Reserve Bank of India swap and the Greater Malé Connectivity Project. China holds about 40% of external debt, about $1.37bn, and signed a limited 2024 military assistance agreement for non-lethal equipment and training with no basing rights. Muizzu describes his doctrine as "Maldives First" — balancing both while diversifying to Gulf capital — not a wholesale pivot. On the wider Indian Ocean, the Carnegie Endowment's Mapping the Indian Ocean Region paper lists the UAE as one of several emerging players alongside Saudi Arabia, Türkiye and Russia — with China as the top emerging player — distinct from long-standing traditional players India, the United States, France, Australia, the United Kingdom and Japan. That framing supports analysts' description of a significant but selective UAE footprint rather than uniform dominance, and implications for basing and maritime awareness remain contested. Detailed claims that the UAE is a strategic partner on every Indian Ocean coast, or that specific commercial port holdings prove littoral-wide dominance, could not be independently verified. What is clear is that two Gulf pledges each larger than GDP would, if built, re-anchor external financing toward Abu Dhabi and Dubai alongside New Delhi and Beijing.

What to watch by end-October is concrete: whether the definitive agreement locks $12bn or $20bn as committed capital and discloses how much is Eagle Hills equity versus debt and contingent pre-sales; whether escrow inflows move through Maldivian banks without widening the parallel premium; and whether a marine monitoring plan with enforcement power is published for the full lagoon, not just the 500-hectare parcel.
