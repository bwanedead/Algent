On Sept 17, 2026, the U.S. State Department approved a possible $24.3 billion sale of 48 F-35A stealth fighters to Saudi Arabia and notified Congress for a 30-day review. If concluded, it would make Saudi Arabia the first Arab state to fly America's most advanced stealth fighter — Israel is currently the only operator in the Middle East — and it tests two separate American promises: a law that guarantees Israel's military edge, and a warning from U.S. intelligence that the same jets could expose stealth secrets to China. Whether either holds depends on classified mitigations no outsider can verify and on whether Congress tries to block the deal.

*How 48 F-35s would change Saudi Arabia's fighter fleet, and how that lot compares to the worldwide F-35 program. Adding 48 F-35As would take the Royal Saudi Air Force from about 385 combat aircraft (232 F-15s, 72 Typhoons, 81 Tornados) to 433 if those types all stay — a 12.5% increase, or about 15% of the 304 fighters that would remain if the Tornados retire. The same 48-jet lot is smaller than Israel's 75 ordered F-35Is (42–45 already flying) and the UAE's 2020 notice for 50 (none delivered), and it is about 4% of the 1,200-plus F-35s already operating worldwide and 2% of the 2,454 aircraft the United States plans to buy. The jet at the center is the F-35 Lightning II, America's most advanced stealth fighter, built to evade radar, fuse sensor data and fight networked. The buyer is the Royal Saudi Air Force, which today flies mostly American F-15s, European Typhoons and aging Tornados it wants to replace. The process is a Foreign Military Sale, the government-to-government system in which the State Department approves a possible package, Congress gets a review window, and only then do the U.S. and the buyer negotiate a final contract. Approval is not delivery.

## What $24.3 billion actually buys

The notice, Transmittal No. 26-69, lists 48 F-35A aircraft — the conventional-takeoff version that uses normal runways — and 49 Pratt & Whitney F135-PW-100 engines, 48 installed plus one spare. The rest is the long tail that makes a stealth fleet work: key loaders, cryptographic appliques, secure communications and navigation gear, electronic-warfare database support, spares, repair and return, training aids and simulators, software and publications, ferry and refueling, site surveys and U.S. government engineering support.

That is why the price looks so high. The $24.3 billion is an estimated total package, not a sticker price for 48 airframes and not a final contract. The same figure — $24.3 billion — was the price for 296 F-35 airframes alone in the Lots 18-19 production contract definitized in September 2025, at an average of about $82 million per airframe before engines. The Saudi package works out to roughly $506 million per aircraft once engines, spares, training and support are included, which is typical for a Foreign Military Sale. The State Department said there is no known offset agreement and no additional U.S. personnel would be required in Saudi Arabia.

Scale helps place it. Forty-eight jets would be about a 15% increase to Saudi Arabia's roughly 385 fighters, but about 2% of the planned U.S. program of 2,454 aircraft and about 4% of the more than 1,200 F-35s already operating from 48 bases and 12 ships in 20 nations. At the recent production pace of 191 deliveries in 2025, 48 jets is roughly a third of a year's output. First deliveries, if the sale survives review and contracting, would still be years away; no lot assignment or delivery date was published.

## The legal promise: 'will not alter the military balance'

Every U.S. arms notice for the region carries the same line, and this one did too: the proposed sale "will not alter the military balance in the region."

That sentence is not a press line. It is the public face of a classified test required by U.S. law. Since the Naval Vessel Transfer Act of 2008, codified as Section 36(h) of the Arms Export Control Act (22 USC 2776(h)), the president must ensure that arms exports to the Middle East do not adversely affect Israel's Qualitative Military Edge — defined in law as Israel's ability to counter and defeat credible conventional threats from any state, coalition or non-state actor while sustaining minimal damage, through superior weapons and other capabilities in sufficient quantity. Assessments are required biennially since 2013.

The analysis itself is classified and interagency — State, the Defense Security Cooperation Agency, the Joint Staff, the intelligence community and combatant commands — with Israeli input through the Joint Political Military Group. After the Sept 17 transmittal, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said Secretary of State Marco Rubio had reiterated in a phone call that Washington is committed to preserving Israel's edge "in everything related to supplying weapons and military systems to countries in the Middle East" and that the advantage is legally guaranteed. U.S. officials indicated Saudi jets would be less advanced than Israel's and limited to two squadrons.

## Why that promise is contested

The same sentence that satisfies the law is also why many analysts do not trust it. The "will not alter the balance" language is routine boilerplate that appears in every DSCA notification — for Taiwan, Pakistan, Greece and others — intended to pre-empt congressional objections, not an independently verifiable finding.

The Israeli Air Force itself submitted a formal position paper warning that a Saudi acquisition of up to 48 F-35s could "erode or significantly degrade" Israel's edge and regional air superiority, citing the jet's advanced detection, data-processing and networked systems.

Independent analysts made the same point in blunter terms. Eric Mandel assessed that "even a slightly downgraded fifth-generation F-35 in Saudi hands would be a strategic game-changer" and that Israeli Air Force and U.S. intelligence and defense officials believe it would undermine Israel's dominance of regional airspace. Yoel Guzansky, head of the Gulf program at Israel's Institute for National Security Studies, said "The state of Israel's QME is not as good as it was before Trump's decision" and warned of an arms race with Qatar, the United Arab Emirates and Turkey. Former national security adviser Yaakov Amidror said a jet in another Middle East country's hands "will be problematic, because we will lose some uniqueness." A Hudson Institute assessment called the sale a "turning point for airpower dynamics in the Middle East."

Washington's answer, as reported rather than confirmed in the unclassified notice, is a bundle of mitigations that could square the circle without breaking the law. Saudi jets would be downgraded relative to Israel's F-35I Adir — Israel flies the only F-35 variant contractually authorized for deep indigenous integration, with its own electronic-warfare suite, command-and-control, weapons and software, and independent sustainment. Israel has ordered 75 F-35Is and operates 42 to 45 of the first 50, with the rest due through 2028. Other reported mitigations include capping the sale at two squadrons, barring basing at western Saudi airfields closest to Israel, compensating Israel with additional capabilities, and stretching deliveries over five to ten years. None of those details are confirmed in the public transmittal, and the classified QME analysis cannot be independently checked.

That creates the perverse incentive critics flag. Compensating Israel to preserve the legal edge can fuel a regional arms race — in tension with the Arms Export Control Act's own policy to discourage arms races — while denying or limiting the sale to preserve the edge can push Gulf buyers toward China or Russia, which are not bound by QME at all.

## A separate warning: China and the stealth secrets

Distinct from the Israel debate is the intelligence community's objection. Assessments from the Defense Intelligence Agency in fall 2025 and broader intelligence reporting in September 2026, reported by The New York Times, warned that Beijing could gain access to sensitive F-35 technology through traditional espionage in Saudi Arabia or through deepening Saudi-China military and telecom ties. Saudi telecom infrastructure heavily uses Chinese equipment, and China has helped Saudi missile production, including solid-rocket fuel and past sales of DF-3 and DF-21 ballistic missiles, plus Wing Loong II and CH-4 drones.

The DIA warning is a risk assessment, not an established breach, and it does not assert that specific U.S. controls have been decided. Proponents of the sale make the opposite case: supplying the jets with U.S. monitoring is preferable to pushing Riyadh toward Chinese or Russian alternatives, and Saudi F-35s would be cloud-dependent and less capable than Israel's independently sustained Adirs.

## The check that still matters: 30 days in Congress

Approval did not make the sale final. The DSCA notification triggered a 30-day congressional review period — 15 days for some NATO-plus cases, 30 for Saudi Arabia. Congress can introduce a joint resolution of disapproval; passage requires simple majorities, but a presidential veto then requires two-thirds in both chambers to override, a historically high bar.

Most arms sales are uncontroversial. An F-35 sale to Saudi Arabia draws greater scrutiny, especially after the intelligence warnings, plus longstanding human-rights and Yemen concerns. No resolution has been filed and no whip count published. The outcome remains open.

## Why Riyadh wants it now, and why Washington wants to sell

For Saudi Arabia, the jets would replace about 81 Tornado IDS aircraft being phased out and complement about 232 F-15s and 72 Typhoons, under the Vision 2030 modernization drive. Drivers cited are deterrence against Iran and renewed fighting with the Houthis, and interoperability with U.S. and NATO forces.

Timing follows politics. President Donald Trump announced in November 2025, ahead of a White House meeting with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, that he would approve F-35s for Saudi Arabia, alongside a Strategic Defense Agreement that also included about 300 M1 Abrams tanks. A full-scale F-35A mockup in Saudi markings was displayed at the World Defense Show in February 2026. Biden-era talks that linked jets to a broader U.S.-Saudi security pact and potential Saudi-Israeli normalization stalled after the Oct. 7, 2023 Hamas attack and the Gaza war.

For Washington, the sale is also about outcompeting China and Russia and building an anti-Iran architecture. The precedent cuts both ways. In November 2020 the Trump administration notified Congress of 50 F-35s for the United Arab Emirates, a $23.37 billion package with MQ-9Bs and munitions offered as an unwritten sweetener for joining the Abraham Accords — the U.S.-brokered deals since 2020 in which Arab states normalized relations with Israel. That sale was paused under President Joe Biden and has not delivered as of 2026.

Israeli officials have reportedly wanted the Saudi sale conditioned on Riyadh joining the Accords, while Saudi Arabia has demanded a credible path to a Palestinian state. The sale as notified is not explicitly conditioned on normalization, and Trump did not tie it to a peace deal even as he urged the crown prince toward one. Approving without that condition, critics warn, forfeits leverage that made the UAE offer meaningful and increases proliferation pressure as Qatar, the UAE and Turkey cite the precedent. Saudi Arabia, meanwhile, hedges with alternatives including a Boeing F-15EX upgrade, interest in Turkey's Kaan fighter, and continued Chinese missile cooperation.

If the deal proceeds, Saudi Arabia would be the first Arab F-35 operator and the second in the Middle East after Israel. Whether the jets arrive downgraded, distant from Israel's border, and late enough to preserve a legal edge — and whether U.S. controls keep their secrets from Beijing — will be decided in the classified details and the next 30 days on Capitol Hill, not in the boilerplate.
