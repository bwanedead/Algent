A European spacecraft will skim past Earth on 28 September, 8,640km over the Indian Ocean, stealing about 3.5km/s and a 20-degree bend without burning much fuel. It is Juice, the Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer built by the European Space Agency (ESA), the intergovernmental agency that builds Europe's science missions. A gravity assist like this uses a planet's motion to slingshot a probe outward; without it a direct flight to Jupiter would need enormous fuel to get there and then brake. The flyby proves Europe can still navigate deep space alone. Whether it can launch, stay connected and navigate without leaning on SpaceX and US goodwill is the harder test of the next five years.

### Why dependence became leverage

For Europe the urgency is not industrial policy. It is whether wartime communications, intelligence and navigation can be switched off or bargained away.

On 24 February 2022, hours before Russia's full invasion of Ukraine, a Russian cyberattack on Viasat's KA-SAT satellite broadband network knocked tens of thousands of modems offline across Ukraine and the EU, requiring factory swaps and what Western officials called a huge loss in communications at the war's outset.

In September 2022 Elon Musk refused a Ukrainian emergency request to activate Starlink coverage over Sevastopol in Crimea to enable a drone attack on Russia's Black Sea Fleet, saying SpaceX would be explicitly complicit in a major act of war; the drones lost connectivity and washed ashore.

In February 2025 Reuters reported, citing three sources, that US negotiators pressing Kyiv for critical minerals access raised cutting Ukraine's Starlink access as leverage — Musk denied it and Reuters stood by the reporting. In spring 2025 the Trump administration withheld US military and commercial Maxar satellite data from Ukrainian forces as pressure to negotiate, an episode cited by German and US think-tanks as political instrumentalisation of space.

The doctrine Europe now repeats — "autonomy where it matters, partnerships where they work" — comes from that sequence. Space is treated as a contested battlespace, not a commercial convenience.

### What a broadband network does not fix

Rockets and broadband are the visible parts. Analysts at Germany's SWP think-tank and at the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS), a Washington research institute, frame dependence more widely: four core defence capabilities — communications, intelligence, navigation and missile early warning — plus two enablers, launch and space situational awareness, the tracking of what is in orbit and whether it threatens satellites.

Europe leans most on the US for the last three. For situational awareness it uses ground radars and its EU Space Surveillance and Tracking network but still relies in part on US data for operational warnings — illustrated, SWP notes, when a Russian satellite shadowed a German military communications satellite in September 2025.

For navigation Europe has Galileo, its own 24-satellite alternative to US GPS in medium orbit thousands of kilometres up. With fewer satellites, CSIS assesses, accuracy drops, signal gaps grow and failure risks rise if GPS were denied.

IRIS², the EU's planned secure satellite network for government use, helps with secure communications. It does not by itself give Europe independent eyes, early warning or a GPS backup at full strength. Fragmentation makes it harder: ESA's principle of geographic return, allocating contracts in proportion to member contributions, is cited as reinforcing inefficiency.

IRIS² is useless if Europe cannot put it up.

Europe flew a handful of times in 2025 — four Ariane 6 and three Vega C flights — versus about 170 SpaceX launches that year, roughly half of all orbital launches globally, putting about 2,500 satellites in orbit. The majority of European satellites now launch on SpaceX.

Ariane 6, financed by ESA member states and built to reach geostationary orbit far above Earth, is still ramping at about 7-8 flights a year, with an ambition of 9-10. ESA Director General Josef Aschbacher, who leads the agency, has warned of a shortage around 2029-2031 as demand peaks from EU institutional missions, EUMETSAT — Europe's weather-satellite agency — science, defence and commercial backlogs. Thirty Ariane 6 launches are already booked, most of them for Amazon's Kuiper broadband constellation, now called Amazon Leo.

ESA is studying whether 12 flights in 2029 is doable with manageable investment, and then 15 or even 20 a year by about 2030. Those higher figures are studied options, not a committed plan. The structural reason is market size: Europe's satellite market is about 1% of the global launch market, SWP estimates, so demand must be consolidated to make a competitive launch market at all. Without Starlink-like internal demand — SpaceX flew well over a hundred Falcon 9s in 2023 versus Ariane 6's single inaugural flight — the economics of reusable rockets do not close. Early efforts to nurture small launchers are too soon to assess. The gap has already forced workarounds: after Ariane 5 retired, Soyuz was lost after the invasion of Ukraine and Vega C was grounded, ESA launched its Euclid and EarthCARE science missions on Falcon 9.

IRIS² is Europe's concrete answer for government use — crisis response, defence, border surveillance, embassies — not a consumer broadband play. The concession was awarded 31 October 2024 and signed 16 December 2024 between the European Commission, the EU executive, and SpaceRISE, a consortium of SES, Eutelsat and Hispasat. ESA oversees development and in-orbit validation, with Airbus, Thales Alenia Space, OHB and Aerospacelab in the industrial team.

The design grew after negotiations from January to August 2026 from about 282 satellites to 348 — most in low orbit a few hundred kilometres up where latency is low, plus a smaller medium-orbit layer for wider coverage — after adding satellites for defence resilience. ESA says that lifts secure governmental capacity by about 60% within the EU and more than half globally. First launches are targeted for 2029, with progressive services from 2029 onwards — targeted, not firm.

Total investment is now reported at more than €15.6bn — about €11.6bn public plus about €4bn private from SpaceRISE — up from €10.55bn at concession, a rise of about 50%. That figure and the assessment that the "acceleration" to 2029 amounts to a handful of months come from European Spaceflight reporting, not an ESA release.

Starlink's moat is not just satellites. It is the terminal. Starlink began 2026 with about 9,500 working satellites, approval from the US regulator the FCC for another 7,500, about 9.2 million users in roughly 155 countries and a de facto monopoly in consumer low-orbit broadband. Its vertical integration — reusable Falcon 9 and planned Starship, mass-produced satellites and dishes — supports plans around $0.30-0.50 per Mbps with further price erosion expected.

The counter-bet is standards. Sharp, the Japanese electronics maker, is pitching an antenna that can connect to multiple networks so users can switch providers instead of being locked to Starlink's proprietary dish, by adapting global mobile-phone standards known as 3GPP so phones and terminals can talk directly to satellites.

In November 2025 at ESA's technical centre in the Netherlands, ESA, chipmaker MediaTek, Eutelsat, Airbus, Sharp and others demonstrated what they called the world's first 3GPP Release-19 5G-Advanced connection over low-orbit satellites, using OneWeb satellites built by Airbus and a Sharp flat-panel antenna. If that stack becomes ubiquitous, any compatible constellation — OneWeb, IRIS², Kuiper — could complement terrestrial 5G and let phones, cars and sensors switch networks. Sharp has not published specs, timeline, pricing or customers. The demo is proven; scale and cost are not. Industry analysis is blunt: Via Satellite, quoting consultant Carlos Placido, says sustainable competition will require differentiated ecosystems rather than undercutting Starlink on cost per bit, and an IEEE Communications Society analysis concludes standardized 5G satellite links "cannot independently offset those scale advantages" — IRIS² remains materially behind Starlink in deployed scale, launch capacity, terminals and service maturity and should be judged as sovereign resilience, not a like-for-like retail challenger.

### The slingshot that still works

Juice is the counterpoint. It launched 14 April 2023 from Europe's spaceport in French Guiana on an Ariane 5, carrying 10 science instruments and 85 sq m solar wings. After the first-ever lunar-Earth flyby in August 2024 and a Venus flyby in August 2025, the 28 September 2026 Earth flyby sets up a final Earth flyby in January 2029 for Jupiter arrival in July 2031. It will then make 35 flybys of the ocean-bearing moons Ganymede, Callisto and Europa before becoming the first spacecraft to orbit a moon other than Earth's, Ganymede, in late 2034. The navigation campaign started in mid-August; only one correction burn was needed. The flyby is not without constraint: Juice will pass through Earth's shadow and must run on batteries, so observations are prioritized, with calibrations on Earth and the Moon and magnetotail measurements that can be combined with other missions between late September and early October.

Three headwinds are cited as symmetrically as the ambition. Cost and schedule: the budget growth to more than €15.6bn for a handful of months' acceleration raises fiscal questions, and the 2029 target collides with the rocket bottleneck. Launch capacity: even 12 flights in 2029 leaves IRIS² competing for the same pads as Kuiper and institutional missions, and studying 15-20 flights does not create them. Market maths: reusability only pays with high-frequency low-orbit demand of the kind Starlink creates internally, and standards alone do not erase a cost and scale moat built on about half of global launches and mass-produced terminals.

None of that means IRIS² will fail. It means the outcome is partial and contested: Europe can buy back sovereign resilience for government communications and keep deep-space navigation sovereign, as Juice shows, while remaining dependent on the US for launch surge capacity, for eyes and warning in orbit, and for the cheapest bits in space — unless demand consolidates and terminals truly become interchangeable at Starlink prices, both unproven at scale.
