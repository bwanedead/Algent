A 100-gigawatt-hour battery gigafactory — enough for about 1.25 million electric cars — has started trial runs in eastern Hungary.

On 22 September 2026, Contemporary Amperex Technology Co. Limited — CATL, the Chinese company that makes about 40% of the world's EV batteries — began testing the first two cell lines at its planned 100 GWh plant in Debrecen, after receiving its occupancy permit in August and clearing the preconditions of its EU pollution permit. The lines are not yet making batteries for sale; whether the plant, once ramped, will push European cell prices lower remains conditional, not settled.

That distinction matters because trial production is equipment optimisation and process validation before series production. The start had slipped from a late-2025 to early-2026 target cited by CATL's European general manager Matt Shen, a delay the company links to construction and permitting sequencing rather than demand.

### What actually started in Debrecen

Debrecen is CATL's second European factory after Arnstadt in Germany, and will be its largest outside China when complete. CATL announced the project in August 2022, began building the first cell building in summer 2023 and finished installing production equipment by spring 2026.

The site has already been assembling battery modules — packs of cells wired together — since autumn 2024, making about 537,000 modules while the cell lines were built. County authorities confirmed the company had fixed deficiencies identified earlier in permitting before the August permit. CATL says it will keep monitoring employee health, safety and environmental indicators during trials and submit results to authorities.

What is not yet known is when series production begins or how the 100 GWh will be phased. The company describes flexible lines tailored to customer requirements without disclosing chemistry, customers or pricing.

### How big 100 GWh really is

Against global baselines the scale is system-shaping, but the share depends on the denominator. The plant would be about 5% of global cell manufacturing capacity on the last published total of almost 2,000 GWh in 2023, and about 10-13% of recent EV battery demand, which grew from more than 750 GWh in 2023 to more than 950 GWh in 2024. Until a new global capacity total is published, the capacity share still rests on the 2023 figure.

In European terms the increment is larger. Europe produced 110 GWh of batteries in 2023 against 185 GWh of demand, leaving it import-dependent. Hungary alone accounted for almost 30% of European output and Poland about 60%. A 100 GWh plant in Debrecen would therefore nearly double Hungary's 2023 share of European production and rival total US production of 70 GWh that year. For CATL itself, 100 GWh is about 34% of the 289.6 GWh it installed globally from January to July 2026.

### Why Hungary, why now

The logic is localisation: put cell supply next to car assembly and cut logistics, tariffs and lead times compared with importing from China.

Hungary already hosts a cluster of European auto assembly — BMW, Mercedes and BYD, the Chinese automaker, have invested there — and already produced a large share of Europe's batteries. Debrecen sits near that heartland and near CATL's first European plant in Germany. EU and US policy pushes to build near demand centres form the backdrop, though CATL has not cited those policies as its motive for Debrecen.

The plant's chemistry will shape who it serves. NMC offers higher energy density for premium models and remains dominant in Europe and the US, while LFP is about 30% cheaper per kilowatt-hour and dominant in China. CATL makes both globally; for Debrecen it has only said the lines are flexible.

### The glut it lands in

The trial comes as the battery industry is awash in capacity. In 2023 China used less than 40% of its maximum cell output, while its capacity to make key active materials was several times global EV cell demand. Supply of key battery minerals exceeded demand, pulling down mineral prices.

That overcapacity has fed an asymmetric price fall. Lithium-ion pack prices fell 20% in 2024, the largest drop since 2017, but nearly 30% in China compared with 10-15% in Europe and the US, widening the China-Europe price gap.

Western gigafactories have already felt the squeeze. Northvolt, the Swedish champion once seen as Europe's answer to CATL, filed for bankruptcy protection in late 2024 with $5.8bn in debts after BMW cancelled a €2bn long-term cell contract for late delivery. Its flagship in Skellefteå aimed for 16 GWh a year by 2026, three years late and far below its 60 GWh nameplate.

Whether Debrecen adds to that pressure is not deterministically established. Additional low-cost CATL supply, once ramped, could weigh on European cell prices — helping automakers and buyers but squeezing higher-cost Western plants such as Northvolt and European ventures ACC and Verkor — but that outcome is conditional on chemistry mix, offtake pricing, how fast European demand absorbs the increment, and how fully competitors use their own capacity. Hungarian battery production actually fell significantly in 2024, and Northvolt's utilisation remains far below nameplate, a reminder that demand and execution, not nameplate alone, set prices.

### Who gains, who is exposed — and why the local welcome is contested

If Debrecen ramps smoothly, European automakers get secure local supply and shorter chains, Hungary gains jobs, foreign investment and tax base, CATL gains a tariff hedge and a larger share of the European value pool, and EV buyers could benefit if savings pass through.

The exposure is equally clear and is the core paradox. Geographic localisation is not sovereignty. China controls about 85% of global cell production and, by measures from the International Energy Agency (IEA), nearly 90% of cathode and more than 97% of anode capacity — the two electrodes at the heart of a cell. Core intellectual property, supply chains and profits remain Chinese-controlled. Former European Central Bank president Mario Draghi warned in September 2024 about green-tech competition from China, and analysts have warned that if Europe cannot scale its own cathode-material and cell production, it will become heavily dependent on Asia. Debrecen adds European capacity while entrenching that technology dependence.

Local legitimacy is the other contested edge. Residents and NGOs raised serious worries during permitting, triggering inspections and remediation before the August permit. Concerns centre on water use and monitoring. CATL reports water-saving upgrades and an expanded monitoring network, with a connection to reuse treated wastewater — grey water — planned for summer 2027. Independent experts identified shortcomings in the pollution permit and litigation was pending as of May 2025 with suspension risk. Even with compliance, the social licence remains contested and will depend on ongoing monitoring that CATL says it will submit proactively.

Debrecen therefore holds three truths at once: a genuine industrial win that localises supply next to Europe's auto heartland, a possible price pressure that could help carmakers while hurting their would-be European cell suppliers, and a strategic dependence that grows even as the factory rises on European soil. How those balance will turn on details CATL has not disclosed — chemistry, phasing, customers and pricing — and on whether European demand growth absorbs what the plant will eventually make.
