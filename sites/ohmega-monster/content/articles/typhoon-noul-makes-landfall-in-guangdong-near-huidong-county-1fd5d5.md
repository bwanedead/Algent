Typhoon Noul made landfall along the coast of Huidong county in Huizhou, Guangdong, at about 3:50 a.m. local time on 26 July, according to China’s national weather center as reported by AP. After landfall, the storm moved inland and weakened.

Different agencies were tracking different parts of the same event. The Hong Kong Observatory’s track bulletin is an observation summary of the storm’s intensity over time, while mainland weather authorities were the source for the landfall statement; transport operators and local authorities then made safety decisions from those forecasts and warnings.

The Hong Kong Observatory’s track bulletin shows the storm’s observed intensity easing through the morning. It lists Noul as a severe typhoon at 02:00 HKT, a typhoon at 08:00 HKT, and a severe tropical storm at 10:00 HKT, with maximum sustained winds near the center falling from 155 km/h to 120 km/h and then 110 km/h. That sequence shows the storm weakening, but it does not settle the mainland official landfall classification or landfall intensity.

AP said more than 340,000 people had been relocated for safety in Guangdong by Saturday afternoon, train services in the province were canceled for Sunday, and more than 150 flights were canceled at Hong Kong airport. AP also reported a scaffolding collapse on a high-rise building in Hong Kong; police said initial reports indicated there were no injuries. Those are reported figures, not independently verified totals in this run.

Weather concern extended beyond the coast. China’s National Meteorological Center expected parts of southern Guangdong and southeastern Fujian to experience heavy rain and strong winds over the weekend. Reuters likewise said mainland weather authorities expected Noul to make landfall along the southern coast before moving inland and gradually weakening, with Guangdong set to begin suspending rail services on Saturday.

Vietnam belongs in the story only as a secondary forecast-monitoring zone. Vietnam’s National Center for Hydro-Meteorological Forecasting said the southern region of Vietnam and Lam Dong province could see 20–40 mm of rain, with isolated totals above 100 mm, as Noul moved inland and weakened.

What remains unconfirmed here is the exact mainland landfall intensity, verified post-landfall rainfall totals in China, and whether Noul caused casualties, outages, port closures, or other significant damage. The evidence base is enough to confirm the landfall sequence and the reported disruption footprint, but not enough to treat every headline figure as independently established.
