The Florida Chamber Foundation says Florida’s economy is worth $1.8 trillion, has grown 6.3% over the past year, and now ranks as the world’s 14th-largest economy. In the Chamber’s telling, that is evidence for a larger policy argument: that Florida’s low-tax, low-regulation, free-enterprise model has worked and should keep being treated as the right direction for the state. The Chamber says the milestone means Florida has moved past Australia and Mexico and is part of the Florida 2030 Blueprint’s push to make the state a top 10 global economy by 2030; Mark Wilson, who leads both the Florida Chamber of Commerce and the Chamber Foundation, calls it further proof that free enterprise works.

Fox Business repeated the same ranking and quoted Wilson using it as evidence that Florida’s model is working.

The statistical base is real too. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes an official GDP-by-state series, and it defines state GDP as a comprehensive measure of the value of goods and services produced in a state. BEA also says the state tables are available in its interactive data system and downloadable in standard formats.

What this pass does not fully close is the Chamber’s exact calculation. The Chamber release in hand does not show the underlying BEA table, the external country-GDP dataset, the exchange-rate treatment, or the tie-and-rounding rules used to produce the No. 14 ranking. So the headline is methodologically intelligible — state GDP compared with sovereign-economy nominal GDP is a standard kind of size benchmark, and the World Bank and IMF both publish country-level nominal GDP series for that purpose — but the Chamber’s specific ranking method is not reproducible from the text read here alone.

Even if the number checks out, it does not answer the larger question of how Floridians are actually doing. Nominal GDP is a measure of size. It is not a measure of affordability, household costs, income distribution, insurance pressure, or whether residents feel secure in the economy they live in. The Chamber’s own release notes that its upcoming mid-year report will examine housing affordability, inflation, workforce and other pressures, which is a reminder that the aggregate headline does not settle the lived-economy debate.

Florida appears to have a very large economy by nominal output, and the Chamber is entitled to advertise that scale. But the rank by itself does not prove that the state’s policy model has delivered broad-based well-being for residents.
