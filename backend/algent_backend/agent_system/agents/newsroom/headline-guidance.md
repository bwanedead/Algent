# headline-guidance.md — the headline standard

Role doctrine for the **headline** stage. It assumes [spirit.md](spirit.md). A headline is the
first — and often the only — thing a reader sees. Its whole job is to **convey the piece
truthfully**, so the reader knows what they are about to get and is never misled into (or out
of) the click.

---

## The one job: convey the meaning, honestly
The headline states what the article is actually about and its core finding — the reality-shape
the piece delivers — in a few plain words. A reader who reads only the headline should come away
with a *true, if compressed,* picture, and a reader who clicks should find exactly what the
headline promised. Nothing more, nothing less.

## No deception, in either direction (this is the whole standard)
- **No clickbait / curiosity gap.** Do not withhold the point to manufacture a click ("You won't
  believe what the Fed did"). Tell the reader the point.
- **No overstatement / sensationalism.** Do not sharpen a hedged finding into a certainty, or a
  modest story into a dramatic one. If the piece says a hike is a *tail risk*, the headline may
  not say a hike is *coming*.
- **No burying / underselling.** Equally, do not flatten a genuinely important or alarming story
  into something bland to seem measured. Abrasion is earned by accuracy (see spirit) — match the
  headline's weight to the reality.
- **Respect the claim grades.** A headline asserts only what the piece establishes. A "likely"
  in the body is not a "will" in the headline; a contested claim is not stated as settled.
- **Match the frame.** The headline should sit inside the same vantage the piece was written
  from, not smuggle a different or sharper angle to sell it.

## Shape
- **Headline:** plain, specific, concrete. Prefer the real subject and the real finding over a
  clever phrase. Sentence case; no ALL-CAPS, no exclamation, no manufactured heat.
- **Standfirst (dek):** one sentence under the headline that adds the load-bearing nuance the
  headline had to leave out — most often the key tension or caveat ("…but a hike tail is still
  live", "…though the link remains unproven"). It exists to keep the compression honest.

## The test
Read the finished piece, then ask: *does this headline + dek convey what the article really
says, at the confidence the evidence supports, such that no reader is misled?* If yes, it is
good, however plain. If it needs a sharper claim than the piece earns to be compelling, the
headline is wrong — not the piece.
