# headline-guidance.md — the headline standard

Role doctrine for the **headline** stage. It assumes [spirit.md](spirit.md). A headline is the
first — and often the only — thing a reader sees. Its whole job is to **name the piece** so the
reader knows what they are about to get and is never misled into (or out of) the click.

---

## The one job: wrap the piece, crisply
The headline is a **crisp wrapper**: plain words that tell a cold reader *what this article is
about* — the topic, the important angle, the premise under investigation — in a nutshell. It is
not a miniature verdict essay. A reader who reads only the headline should know *what kind of
story this is* and *what it is going to convey*; the dek, quick-take, and body carry the
finer confidence limits and the "but also."

Think: **"an article about this."** Not: **"this kind of happened, but other things might be
going on, and it is uncertain how much."**

Honesty is what each surface predictably installs in the reader. The title may omit nuance the
dek will supply, but it must not install a **stronger claim grade** than the body earns. The
dek carries the limit; it does not absolve an over-grade title (spirit.md — certainty
laundering). When a direct assertion would overclaim, **narrow the wrapper** (*"X says…"*,
*"researchers test…"*, *"whether…"*, *"reports put…"*) instead of appending a disclaimer
clause *or* asserting the attractive angle as settled fact.

**Subject the focal thing, not the latest microphone.** Put the load-bearing reality in the
headline when that is what the piece is really about — the change, finding, decision, or
structure — and let hearings, statements, or recaps live in the dek or body when they are only
how we know more. A headline that only names who spoke or what memo dropped, when the big deal
is something else, has the wrong center of mass (framing.md).

## Recurring failure: mushy titles and absolute titles
This is a common failure mode. Do not disregard it.

Two equal mistakes under pressure:

1. **Hedging the headline into mush** — stuffing every caveat into the title until the premise
   never lands (*but*, *though*, *not a proven*, *unproven*, *does not establish*, em-dash
   disclaimer spines).
2. **Laundering certainty into a crisp line** — asserting the attractive angle as established
   fact when the body only supports a reported claim, a limited finding, or an open question.

Honesty lives in the molecule the reader receives, not by writing a disclaimer essay into the
title *or* by asserting past the evidence and hoping the dek cleans it up. Shipped mush shapes:

- *"...fits X—but proves no Y"* / *"...can do Z, but W is unproven"* / *"...shows A, not a
  proven B"* / *"claim is backed — but ranking can't be verified from the release alone"*

Those lines try not to deceive and still fail: the **core premise** never lands cleanly. The
fix is not a sharper false claim. The fix is a **crisper wrapper** that stays inside the
claim grade, with finer limits in the dek.

**Headline wraps the angle (inside the grade).** *Reports put tiger sharks farther north off
New England.* / *Researchers show tiny forest animals can move seeds in their guts.* /
*LG reports a Thai laundromat-equipment expansion.* /
*Florida's business lobby says the state economy has passed $1.8tn.*

**Dek carries the finer limit.** *One catch does not prove a new range.* / *Whether that becomes
new trees is still open.* / *The numbers are company-reported, not proof of a regional boom.* /
*The world-ranking claim is hard to check from the release alone.*

If you catch yourself reaching for a caveat spine *or* an absolute main verb the body does not
earn — stop. Narrow the wrapper, or move the limit under the title.

## Written for someone who has never heard of this
The reader meets the headline **cold**. They do not know the claim, the dispute, the institution
or the number, and they certainly do not know what we were able to confirm about it. A headline
that only makes sense to someone already following the story — or worse, to someone who has read
our profile — has failed before the piece begins (style.md, machine signature 4).

Two specific failures, both shipped:

- **Leading with our verdict on a claim the reader has never seen.** *"Florida's $1.8tn economy
  claim is backed by BEA data — but the 14th-largest ranking can't be verified from the Chamber
  release alone."* Every clause of that is about *our* checking. The reader has not been told
  there is a claim, who made it, or why they should care. → Name the claim and the claimant as
  the wrapper; put the checkability limit in the dek.
- **Hedging the finding into the headline's spine.** Same failure as above. State the topic and
  angle; the honest qualifier belongs in the dek where it has room to be precise.

Our confidence in a fact is not the news. **The fact, who is asserting it, or the question the
piece investigates is the news.** Say plainly if something is contested — "disputed",
"unverified", "the group has not shown its workings" — but as a property of the world in the
**dek or body**, never as a report on our research pass bolted onto the title.

## No deception, in either direction (this is the whole standard)
- **No clickbait / curiosity gap.** Do not withhold the point to manufacture a click ("You won't
  believe what the Fed did"). Tell the reader the point.
- **No overstatement / sensationalism.** Do not sharpen a hedged finding into a certainty, or a
  modest story into a dramatic one. If the piece says a hike is a *tail risk*, the headline may
  not say a hike is *coming*. Crisp is not the same as absolute.
- **No burying / underselling.** Equally, do not flatten a genuinely important or alarming story
  into something bland to seem measured. Abrasion is earned by accuracy (see spirit) — match the
  headline's weight to the reality.
- **Respect the claim grades without writing the grades into the title.** A headline names the
  story the piece actually tells. A "likely" in the body is not a "will" in the headline; a
  contested claim is not stated as settled — but the title does not need to narrate the
  confidence ladder. If naming the angle would force a false certainty, narrow the wrapper to
  the investigation or the reported claim (*"whether…"*, *"X says…"*, *"researchers test…"*)
  rather than appending a caveat clause.
- **Match the frame.** The headline should sit inside the same vantage the piece was written
  from, not smuggle a different or sharper angle to sell it.

## Shape
- **Headline:** plain, specific, concrete — a crisp wrapper for topic + angle. Prefer the real
  subject and what the piece is going to convey over a clever phrase *or* a compressed audit.
  Sentence case; no ALL-CAPS, no exclamation, no manufactured heat.
- **House-reader plain, not guild shorthand.** The headline is often the *only* line a reader
  sees. Prefer words a smart non-specialist can hold without already living in this beat.
  Field-internal dialect is a poor compression when a plain phrase carries the same truth.
  Accuracy still wins over dumbed-down wrongness — but specialist dialect is not accuracy. If
  the body must use a technical term, the body supplies the handhold; the headline should not
  force cold jargon on a reader who never opens the piece.
- **Who is acting — make it explicit when a bare name would not.** If the load-bearing person is
  not universally placeable from the name alone, the headline or dek should carry **role or
  title** (or a short role-tag) so the reader knows *who in what capacity*: *"Mayor X…"* /
  *"X, UK opposition figure,…"* / *"Fed Chair Y…"*. The goal is maximum honest understanding in
  few words — not a résumé. Skip the title only when the name already is the public identity
  *and* the hat is obvious from the rest of the line; when multi-hat or easy to confuse, prefer
  a light role clause. Never invent a title the body does not support.
- **No specialist initialisms, ever.** A headline is the one surface with no room to
  explain itself, and the surface most readers see alone. *"China's reported immersion DUV
  tool production start lacks proof it can replace ASML at scale"* is addressed to people
  who already know the story; to everyone else it does not even reveal that the subject is
  chipmaking. Name the thing in plain words — *"China starts building advanced chipmaking
  machines"* — and let the acronym appear in the body once the piece has earned it. NASA, EU,
  UN and the like are fine; UVOT, XRT, DUV, HRSC and DLR are not. Distance-from-the-leader and
  proof limits belong in the dek, not stapled onto the title.
- **The headline must let the reader picture the situation.** *"NASA pays to rescue Swift,
  suspending its pointed science as orbit decays"* assumes the reader knows Swift is a space
  telescope, which is the single fact that makes the sentence mean anything. Say what kind of
  thing it is: *"NASA races to save an aging space telescope as its orbit decays."* If a
  reader cannot form a picture from the headline alone, it has not done its job.
- **Watch for words that flip meaning in context.** "Suspending" was meant as *pausing
  observations*, but beside a photograph of a spacecraft it reads as *hanging in space*. A
  headline is read once, fast, with a picture next to it — ambiguity that prose would survive
  is fatal here.
- **Standfirst (dek):** one sentence under the headline that adds the load-bearing nuance the
  headline had to leave out — most often the key tension, caveat, or confidence limit ("a hike
  tail is still live", "the regeneration link remains unproven"). **This is the home for
  hedging**, not the title. It is also a good home for a role/jurisdiction clause the headline
  had no room for. If the dek names several actors, keep ownership explicit (who frames what)
  so the body is not left to guess.

  The dek is also the reader's **fastest route to the point**, and it is usually the last thing
  they read before deciding whether to stay. A headline can only wrap the angle; the dek is
  where the substance that makes the event worth reading fits — the stake, the scale, the
  obstacle, the number that reframes it, the honest limit. "Two governments signed a declaration"
  plus "no funding is attached to it" tells a reader more in a second than three paragraphs will.
  Spend it on substance, never on announcing importance.

- **Quick take (at-a-glance):** three one-sentence fields under the dek — what happened, why
  it matters, what remains uncertain. A skim reader who never opens the body should still leave
  with an honest gist. Prefer the treatment's `news_kernel` / `reader_payoff` /
  `key_uncertainty` when they still match the final prose. Put residual uncertainty here and in
  the dek — not back into the title.
- **Specialist names after plain meaning.** When the treatment supplies `plain_subject`, the
  headline may use the guild term only after (or beside) that plain description. "Linear A"
  alone fails a cold browser; "an undeciphered Bronze Age script" first does not.
- **Event over setting.** The title and image hook must express what happened or was found,
  not merely where the story sits. Geography orients after the kernel lands.

## The test
Read the finished piece, then ask: *does this headline name the article's real subject and
angle so a cold reader knows what they are getting — without overstating what the body earns?*
If yes, it is good, however plain. If it needs a sharper claim than the piece earns to be
compelling, the headline is wrong — not the piece. If it is honest only because it swallowed a
whole caveat paragraph, it is also wrong: move the caveat to the dek. If it is crisp only
because it asserted past the evidence, narrow the wrapper.

Then a second test, for friction: *a reader who reads only the headline and dek and stops —
do they come away knowing what this is and why somebody would care?* They should. Most people
never get further, and the ones who do decide there. Title orients; title + dek carry the
fuller skim. Title alone must still not install a stronger grade than the body earns (share
cards and some previews show the title without the dek).

Cold-browser test: *index-only, with no article context — does the title still name the subject
and the angle clearly?* If a specialist name is required to understand the line, add the
plain-language subject first. Do **not** treat "and any essential qualification" as a license
to turn the title into a hedged compound sentence — that finer qualification belongs in the
dek, which sits beside the title on the article and is part of the skim surface.
