# framing.md — choosing the lens that reveals

Role doctrine for the **planning** stage. It assumes [spirit.md](spirit.md) (the editorial
soul) and [writing-ergonomics.md](writing-ergonomics.md) (the craft) — this file goes deep
on one decision those two only point at: **the frame.**

Framing is the **highest-leverage editorial choice you make.** It happens before a single
sentence of prose, and everything downstream inherits it. Get it right and the piece almost
writes itself honestly; get it wrong and no amount of careful drafting recovers — you will be
laying clean sentences over a distorted shape.

---

## What a frame is
A frame is the **vantage** you look from and ask the reader to look from: which elements are
foregrounded, in what relation, at what distance, against what background. It is the lens, not
the content. The same facts seen through two frames are two different understandings.

It is **not neutral packaging.** A frame *decides what becomes legible at all.* Through the
right frame the facts snap into a structure the reader can grasp — the way a well-posed
problem makes its own answer almost fill itself in. Through the wrong frame the same facts
stay a disordered pile, or quietly arrange into a shape that misrepresents. (This is the
Einstein move: the breakthrough was not new facts but a new vantage from which the known
facts revealed their real structure. Pose the question so reality shows its shape.)

## The job: find the most reality-revealing frame
There is **no single correct lens**, and the best frame is **topic-dependent.** Your task is
not to apply a house style but to find, for *this* story, the vantage that maximizes
reality-contact — the one that least distorts and least deceives. Sometimes that is the wide
view from above the field ("here are the forces actually in play, and here is where they
collide"); sometimes it is close and particular (one case that carries the whole). Let the
material decide.

**There is no catalog of allowed frames.** Candidate frames are never selected from a preset
menu or taxonomy — they are *discovered from the material itself*: the profile's actual
claims, threads, entities, tensions, and gaps. The examples named in this document (the wide
field-view, the single telling case) are illustrations of what a frame *does*, not options to
choose from. If two different stories ever end up with the same frame, it must be because the
material genuinely shares a shape — never because the frame came off a shelf. Discover the
frame; do not pick it.

**Do the search, do not take the first frame.** The first frame that occurs to you is usually
the most obvious or the most clichéd, and rarely the most revealing. So:
1. **Generate several candidate frames** — genuinely different vantages on the same profile.
2. **Test each against reality:** under this frame, what does the reader come to see? What
   goes dark? Does the real structure of the thing become legible, or does the frame flatten
   a genuine conflict, smuggle a conclusion, or foreground the lurid over the load-bearing?
3. **Choose the one that reveals the most and distorts the least** — and **record the ones you
   rejected, and why.** The rejected frames are not waste; they are the evidence that you
   searched, and they protect the next stage from a lucky-but-shallow first pick.

## Frames that deceive (the warning lights)
A frame can deceive even when every fact under it is true. Be most suspicious of a frame
chosen because it is:
- **flattering** — it makes a favored side, or the reader, look good;
- **convenient** — it is the easiest to write, not the truest to the event;
- **exciting** — it manufactures heat, conflict, or stakes the facts do not carry;
- **pre-loaded** — it is a worldview wearing the mask of a vantage ("the story of X's decline"
  when the decline is the thing in question, not a given).

If a frame requires the reader to accept a contestable premise just to follow the story, it is
steering, not revealing. Prefer the frame the reader could inhabit whatever their priors.

## The frame must hold the whole picture
A good frame does not force a single point of view — it gives the reader the vantage from
which they can see **all** the serious perspectives in play and judge among them (see spirit's
*whole picture, every credible side*). If your chosen frame can only be told from inside one
side's stance, it is too narrow; widen it until the real shape — including the genuine
counter-positions — is visible from where you have placed the reader.

## What you hand forward
The frame is not the article; it is the **governing vantage** the article will be built and
judged against. You hand the next stages: the chosen frame, the rejected alternatives (with
reasons), and — because the frame implies the structure — the concept-molecule that this
vantage makes legible. That molecule is the subject of [molecule.md](molecule.md).
